Football laws

What is the backpass rule?

The short answer

The backpass rule stops a goalkeeper handling the ball when a team-mate has deliberately kicked it to him. He may use his feet, chest or head, but not his hands — and the punishment for handling it is an indirect free kick to the opposition, often inside the penalty area.

The rule was introduced in 1992 to end a specific abuse: teams protecting a lead by passing the ball back to the keeper, who would pick it up, hold it, wait for pressure, and roll it out again. It made for tedious, time-wasting football, and the authorities responded by banning the handling of a deliberate kick from a team-mate. Goalkeepers had to learn to play with their feet almost overnight.

The offence has three conditions, and all of them must be met: the ball must have been deliberately played by a team-mate, it must have been played with the foot (a header, a chest pass or a thigh may be handled), and the goalkeeper must handle it with his hands or arms. A deflection, a mis-hit clearance or a tackle that ricochets to the keeper does not count — the law asks whether the team-mate meant to play it there.

The punishment is an indirect free kick from where the keeper handled the ball, unless that is inside the six-yard box, in which case the kick is taken on the six-yard line parallel to the goal line. Because the kick is indirect, the attacking team needs a touch from a second player before it can score, which produces one of football's most tense set pieces: a wall of defenders on the line and a short pass sideways before the shot.

A related rule limits how long the keeper may hold the ball once he legitimately has it in his hands: he must release it within six seconds, and delaying is punished with an indirect free kick as well. Together, the two rules are why modern goalkeepers are expected to be comfortable in possession — the sweeper-keeper role exists largely because the backpass rule made it necessary.

In practice

A defender under pressure rolls the ball back to his keeper, who picks it up with both hands. The whistle goes immediately: indirect free kick to the opposition, eight yards from goal, every defender on the line, one attacker feinting before rolling it sideways for a team-mate to shoot. If the defender had instead headed the ball back, the keeper could legally have caught it — the foot is what makes the difference.

Common misunderstandings

  • The keeper can never handle a pass from a team-mate. Only a deliberate kick. A header, a chest pass, a thigh or any deflection can be handled legally.
  • The backpass offence is a penalty. It is an indirect free kick, and if the handling happened inside the six-yard box the kick is taken on the six-yard line.
  • The six-second rule is never enforced. It is enforced, though referees often warn first. Deliberately holding the ball to waste time is an indirect free kick offence.

Quick questions

Can the keeper handle it if the pass was a header?

Yes. The rule applies only to a deliberate kick with the foot. A deliberate header, chest or thigh pass can be picked up.

What if the ball hits a defender and bounces to the keeper?

That is not a deliberate play, so the keeper may handle it. Referees look for intent from the team-mate, not merely contact.
