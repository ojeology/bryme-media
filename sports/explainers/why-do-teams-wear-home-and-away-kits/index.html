Football language

Why do teams have home and away kits?

The short answer

Home and away kits exist so the two teams can be told apart on the pitch. The home team wears its first-choice colours and, if the referee decides they clash with the visitors', the away team changes — which is why away shirts exist at all, and why some of football's most famous designs were born from a conflict.

The rule is simple and older than professionalism: the two teams must wear colours that distinguish them, and the goalkeepers must wear colours that distinguish them from everyone else. In practice the home club picks its kit first, and if the referee judges there to be a clash, the away club changes. It is the referee's decision, not the clubs', and it is taken before kick-off.

Why the away team changes is convention rather than law — a courtesy to the hosts, and a practical one when you consider that away kits used to be carried in a bag on the morning of the match rather than chosen weeks ahead. Most leagues now require clubs to register three kits a season — first, second and third — precisely so any combination can be resolved without argument on the day.

This is where some of the most loved shirts in football come from. A club forced to change invents a colour it would never have chosen, the team wins, and the design becomes part of its identity — a famous example being England's 1990s away shirts and the many clubs whose second kits are more popular than their first. Third kits exist for the same reason: two away options mean a club can solve a clash whatever its opponent registers.

The details are regulated too. Shirts must have sleeves, numbers must be visible and of a contrasting colour, and competition rules govern the size and placement of badges, sponsors and player names. Undershirts and tape have to match the sleeve colour, and a player who comes on as a substitute must be numbered correctly in the referee's record. Kit rules also cover jewellery and equipment — which is why players are routinely told to remove necklaces before kick-off.

In practice

A club with navy home shirts hosts a team that also plays in blue. The away club registers a yellow second kit, wears it, and wins 3-0 in a match where the shirt is remembered as much as the scoreline. Ten years later the club reissues the same design as a retro shirt, and a colour chosen purely to avoid a clash becomes one of the most successful kits it has ever sold.

Common misunderstandings

  • The away team always changes. Convention says the away team changes when there is a clash, but the referee makes the call and either side can be asked to change.
  • Third kits are purely commercial. They solve clashes that the first two kits cannot — a club needs options for every combination its opponents might register.
  • Goalkeepers can wear the same colours as their team-mates. Keepers must be distinguishable from both teams and from the match officials, which is why their kits are almost always a different colour entirely.

Quick questions

Can a team wear shorts and socks from different kits?

Yes, provided the overall combination distinguishes the teams and the referee is satisfied. Mixed combinations are common when only one element clashes.

Why are goalkeeper kits so different?

The laws require the keeper to be distinguishable from both teams and the officials, so keepers wear colours no outfield player is wearing.
