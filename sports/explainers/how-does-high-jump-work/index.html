How does high jump work?

The short answer

In the high jump, an athlete takes off from one foot and tries to clear a horizontal bar without knocking it down. The bar is raised through a series of heights; competitors may pass a height, but three consecutive failed trials across the competition eliminate a jumper. The athlete who clears the greatest height wins, with the rulebook’s count-back and jump-off procedures used to resolve a tie.

The high jump is a vertical-jump event: athletes run along a curved approach, take off from one foot and cross a bar supported between two uprights. They must clear the bar without dislodging it and land on the mat. The rules do not require the Fosbury Flop or any one style. A jumper may use a technique that suits them, provided it meets the technical rules, including taking off from one foot.

The chief judge announces the starting height and the progression of the bar. At each height, a competitor can attempt a jump or pass. Passing means declining that trial; it is not a failed jump. A pass can be a strategy—for example, a jumper who has used attempts at one height may choose to go straight to a higher one—but they cannot return to a lower height after moving on.

A jumper is eliminated after three consecutive failures, whether those misses occur at one height or across a combination of heights. A successful clearance resets the sequence. This is why a result sheet may show O for a clearance, X for a failure and a dash for a pass. The symbols show the path through the event, while the final mark records the highest height cleared.

The winner is the athlete with the highest successful clearance. If jumpers finish level, the officials first apply count-back: they compare failures at the height last cleared and then total failures through that height. If a tie for first remains, a jump-off normally decides it under World Athletics rules; the event’s regulations set the procedure, and the tied athletes may be allowed to stop and share the place where those rules permit it.

The event is not about jumping from the same spot or reaching the bar with a particular body part. The athlete can choose their approach and clearance style, but the bar, take-off and trial rules are controlled by the officials. The crossbar’s position is checked after each attempt; if it falls because of the athlete’s action, that trial is a failure. If the bar stays up, the clearance is valid even if the landing looks awkward.

A high-jump result is written as a height, not a distance from a take-off board. That makes it different from the long jump, where the nearest mark in the sand determines the measure. The high jump asks how high the athlete can clear; the long jump asks how far they can legally project their landing from the take-off line. Both reward a clean attempt, but their score sheets and failure rules are different.

In practice

A jumper clears 1.90m, passes at 1.93m and begins at 1.96m. The pass is not a miss and the athlete cannot return to 1.93m. If they then fail three attempts at 1.96m, they are out; their recorded result remains the highest height they successfully cleared, 1.90m.

Common misunderstandings

  • A high jumper must go over the bar back-first. The rules permit different clearance styles; the athlete must take off from one foot and clear the bar without knocking it down.
  • Three misses at one height are required for elimination. Three consecutive failures can span different heights. A successful clearance breaks the sequence; a pass is not itself a failed trial.
  • A pass is recorded as a failure. A pass is recorded separately from a failed attempt. It lets the athlete skip that trial, but does not let them return to a lower height later.
  • Every tied high-jump result must end in a jump-off. Officials apply count-back first, and competition regulations can affect how a remaining tie is resolved.

Quick questions

How many attempts are allowed at each high-jump height?

An athlete can have up to three trials at a height, but three consecutive failures across the event eliminate them. Passing a trial is not a failure.

Can a high jumper pass a height?

Yes. A competitor may pass, but after moving on they cannot return to a lower height.

How is a tie in high jump resolved?

Officials use count-back first. A jump-off normally decides a remaining tie for first under World Athletics rules, subject to the event’s regulations and any permitted decision by the tied athletes to stop.
