Football laws

What is a yellow card — and what does it cost?

The short answer

A yellow card is a caution. Two in one match mean a red card and an early bath; across a season they accumulate towards suspensions on a tariff set by each competition. It is the referee's formal record that a player crossed the line — not just a warning.

A caution is the middle rung of football's disciplinary ladder. The referee shows it for unsporting behaviour, dissent, persistent infringement, delaying the restart, failing to retreat at a free kick or corner, entering or leaving the field without permission, and for certain fouls — a reckless tackle, or a professional foul that stops a promising attack. The list is short but the situations are endless, which is why refereeing judgement rather than a checklist decides most cautions.

The immediate consequence is binary: a second yellow in the same match is automatically a red, and the player is dismissed with all that follows. There is no appeal against the arithmetic — two yellows are a red regardless of how soft either one looked. This is why managers sometimes substitute a booked player at half-time; the second card is rarely far behind a player who has already been cautioned and is still competing hard.

Across a season the cards accumulate on a tariff. Most leagues suspend a player for one match after a set number of cautions — five in the Premier League before the cut-off, more afterwards — and the threshold resets at a defined point in the campaign. Bookings also carry fines for clubs in many competitions, and repeat offenders can face larger suspensions from a disciplinary panel rather than an automatic trigger.

There is a crucial distinction fans miss between domestic and international football. In a World Cup or continental tournament, yellow cards accumulate across the group stage and knockouts, and two yellows in different matches usually mean a one-match ban — which is why players who pick up an early caution are sometimes rested for a dead-rubber final group game to clear their record before the knockouts.

In practice

A midfielder is booked in the 20th minute for a late challenge. Every tackle for the next seventy minutes is now judged against the fact that one more card ends his afternoon — so he stands off a runner he would normally have caught, and the manager replaces him at half-time. In a World Cup group stage, the same caution is a different problem entirely: book him again in the second match and he misses the round of sixteen.

Common misunderstandings

  • A yellow card is just a warning with no real cost. Two in a match is a dismissal, and across a season it triggers suspensions and fines on a published tariff.
  • Cards reset at the end of every match. They accumulate across the season (or the tournament) towards suspension thresholds set by each competition.
  • Only fouls get you booked. Dissent, time-wasting, encroachment at free kicks, leaving the pitch without permission and even excessive celebration can all be cautions.

Quick questions

How many yellow cards before a suspension?

It depends on the competition. England uses five bookings before a defined cut-off date in the season, with a higher threshold afterwards; other leagues and tournaments set their own numbers.

Can a yellow card be appealed?

The caution itself generally cannot, but a suspension it triggers can sometimes be appealed if the club argues it was wrongly shown — the same process used to challenge a wrongful dismissal.
