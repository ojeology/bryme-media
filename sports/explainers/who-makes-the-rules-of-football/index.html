Who actually makes the rules of football?

The short answer

The Laws of the Game are written by IFAB — the International Football Association Board — founded in 1886 and made up of the four British associations (England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland) plus FIFA. It is the only body in the world that can change football's rules.

IFAB exists because the rules were born in Britain: the four home associations codified the game in the 19th century and, when FIFA arrived in 1904, it joined their rule-making board rather than creating its own. Each of the four associations holds one vote; FIFA holds four votes. A law change needs a supermajority, which is why no single body — not FIFA, not England — can rewrite football alone.

IFAB meets once a year at its Annual General Meeting, and changes traditionally take effect from 1 June so that competitions around the world start their seasons under the same text. Between AGMs, it runs trials: goal-line technology and VAR were both trialled in live competitions before the board wrote them into the laws, and experiments like concussion substitutes have followed the same path.

The board's decisions shape the game fans argue about every week. The handball rewrites of recent years, the introduction of five substitutions, the added-time clampdown that pushed matches past 100 minutes, and the drawn-out debate about a stop-clock were all IFAB decisions or IFAB-sanctioned trials. Referees do not invent rules, and neither do leagues: they apply the laws IFAB publishes and, where the laws allow, competitions choose options within them.

In practice

Take the addition of video review. The idea was trialled by national federations, tested in live competitions, evaluated by IFAB's technical panels, then written into the Laws of the Game at an AGM. Only after that could the Premier League, La Liga or the AFCON adopt it. Every step in that chain — trial, evaluation, law change, competition adoption — belongs to a different body, which is why rule changes feel slow: they are designed to be.

Common misunderstandings

  • FIFA writes the rules of football. FIFA sits on IFAB with its four votes, but the laws are the board's — and the four British associations each hold a vote that no one can override.
  • Leagues can change rules that suit them. Competitions can choose options the laws allow, like the number of substitutes, but they cannot rewrite the laws themselves. Only IFAB can.

Quick questions

Why do the four British associations get permanent votes?

Because the modern rules were codified in Britain — IFAB is a 19th-century institution that FIFA joined rather than replaced, and the founding associations kept their seats.

How often do the laws actually change?

There is an AGM every year, but most years bring small clarifications rather than big changes. Structural changes like VAR go through multi-year trials first.
