Match mechanics

What is stoppage time — and why is it getting longer?

The short answer

Stoppage time — also called added time — is the period the referee adds to the end of each half to account for time lost to substitutions, injuries, celebrations, video reviews and time-wasting. The number on the board is a minimum: the referee can play longer, and recent clampdowns mean added time is now routinely five to ten minutes a half.

It is the referee's calculation, not a fourth official's. The referee keeps track of stoppages across each half, decides how much to add, and the fourth official displays that number on the board in the final minute — but the number shown is a minimum the referee has decided to play, not a limit. If more time is lost after the board goes up, the referee simply plays on beyond it.

The lengths jumped for a reason. From the 2022-23 season, IFAB and the major leagues pushed referees to count time precisely rather than approximating: every substitution, every goal celebration, every injury treatment, every VAR check and every deliberate delay now goes into the arithmetic. The result is the era of the 100-minute match — matches that used to finish at 93 minutes now finishing at 98 or 102, with the ball in play for perhaps 55 to 60 of them.

That last figure is the deeper story. Studies consistently find the ball is in play for only about 55-60 minutes of a 90-minute match, which is why the phrase 'ball in play time' has become part of the sport's vocabulary. IFAB has discussed more radical fixes — a stop-clock that pauses during dead time, as rugby and basketball use — and some competitions have trialled shorter matches with stopped clocks, but nothing has replaced the referee's judgement at the top of the game yet.

For teams, added time is strategy. A side protecting a lead wants the ball in play; the team chasing wants every second to count. Long added times generally reward the chasing team, which is why the clampdown produced a wave of late goals — and why commentators now watch the board with genuine suspense.

In practice

A tense cup tie has eight substitutions, three goals with long celebrations, a four-minute video review and a goalkeeper who takes the scenic route on every goal kick. The board shows nine minutes. In the tenth added minute a substitute scores the winner, and the debate about where those nine minutes came from runs for a week — subs, celebrations, the review, and forty seconds of walking on every dead ball.

Common misunderstandings

  • The fourth official decides how long to add. The referee decides. The fourth official displays the referee's decision on the board.
  • When the board's time expires, the half ends immediately. The board shows a minimum. Play continues until the referee decides the added period is complete — which is why goals in the tenth minute of three added sometimes stand.
  • Added time only covers injuries. It covers all lost time: substitutions, celebrations, reviews, cooling breaks and deliberate time-wasting.

Quick questions

Why do some matches last more than 100 minutes now?

Because referees were instructed to count lost time strictly instead of rounding it down. The clampdown, in force since 2022-23, routinely pushes matches past 100 minutes total.

Can a goal be scored directly from a corner after added time expires?

Yes — if a corner is awarded before the added period ends, it is taken and the half continues until the attack is complete, which is why keepers sometimes rush forward for one last corner.
