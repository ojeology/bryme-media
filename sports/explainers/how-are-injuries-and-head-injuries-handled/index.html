Match mechanics

How are injuries — and head injuries — handled in football?

The short answer

Treatment happens on the pitch and play usually continues around it, with the referee stopping only when he judges the injury serious, when the head is involved, or when the player needs to leave the field. A player who receives treatment for a head injury must leave the pitch, and a concussion substitute may be used in competitions that have adopted the protocol.

The default is to keep play going. Physios treat minor injuries on the pitch while the game continues, and the referee stops play only when he decides the injury is serious, when the player is on the ground and the ball is near him, or when a head injury is suspected. Once he does stop, the player is usually required to leave the field of play and cannot return until the referee gives permission — which is why a player treated on the touchline jogs back on and waits for a wave.

There is no requirement to stop the clock, which is the source of most of the controversy: the minutes lost are supposed to be added back at the end of the half. That is also why you see players going down to run the clock down, and why referees and, increasingly, video review look closely at whether an injury is genuine. Deliberately faking one to waste time is unsporting behaviour and can be cautioned.

Head injuries are handled under a stricter protocol. If a player is suspected of a concussion or a head injury, play is stopped, the player is assessed by the team doctor, and if a concussion is confirmed or suspected he takes no further part in the match. A player with a suspected concussion must be substituted — he cannot return, even if the assessment later clears him. This is the one area where football's rules have changed most in recent years, driven by evidence about repeated head impacts and the long-term health of players.

To make that safe, IFAB introduced additional concussion substitutes: a team may make an extra substitution specifically for a player with a suspected concussion, and the opposition gets an additional substitution in return, so neither side is disadvantaged. Where a competition has adopted it, the injured player is permanently substituted and the change does not count against the team's normal allocation. Alongside it sit the standard return-to-play protocols — a graduated, medically supervised process over days rather than minutes.

In practice

Two players clash heads and one stays down. The referee stops immediately, the doctor comes on, and after an assessment the player is helped off and takes no further part — even if he is pleading to continue. His team brings on a replacement without using one of its normal substitution windows, and the opposition is granted an extra change too. The whole sequence can take three or four minutes, all of which is added back on at the end.

Common misunderstandings

  • A player who is treated must leave the pitch. Usually yes — if the referee has stopped play for treatment, the player normally has to leave and can return only with permission. Minor treatment during live play is different.
  • A player can carry on if he passes a sideline concussion test. No. If concussion is confirmed or suspected, he takes no further part — the assessment does not license a return to the same match.
  • Head injuries are treated like any other injury. They trigger a specific protocol: stop play, assess, remove the player permanently, and allow an additional concussion substitution where the competition has adopted it.

Quick questions

Does the opposition get an extra substitution too?

Where the concussion substitute protocol is in use, yes — the opposing team is granted an additional substitution so neither side gains an advantage.

Can a player return after treatment?

If play was stopped for his treatment he usually has to leave the field and can only re-enter with the referee's permission, given during a stoppage.
