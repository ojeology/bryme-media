Why is the World Cup every four years?

The short answer

The four-year cycle is a calendar decision, not a law: FIFA spreads the World Cup across a rhythm that leaves room for long qualifying campaigns, continental tournaments like the Euros and Copa América, and the players' club seasons — and the scarcity is exactly what makes the tournament feel like the biggest event in the sport.

The first World Cup was held in 1930 in Uruguay, and every edition since has followed the four-year pattern — with the planned 1942 and 1946 tournaments lost to the Second World War. Four years is long enough for a full qualifying campaign (two years in most confederations), for continental championships to take their own slots, and for the club game to have undisturbed seasons in between.

Scarcity is part of the design. Because a World Cup comes around only four times a decade, careers are measured in them — most players get one or two, the greats perhaps three or four. That reality is what makes qualification agonising and the tournament unmissable, and it is precisely what critics of a two-year cycle pointed to when FIFA floated the idea in the 2020s: playing it more often would risk devaluing the thing itself, on top of overloading players.

The cycle is shared with the other big tournaments, deliberately spread out: the European Championship also runs every four years, the Africa Cup of Nations has settled into a two-year rhythm, and the Copa América has used various cadences but never the World Cup's exact slot on a permanent basis. FIFA's international match calendar — which sets when clubs must release players — is built around these anchors.

Even the format has changed without touching the rhythm: the tournament grew from 16 teams to 32 in 1998 and to 48 for 2026 in North America. So the answer to the question is partly tradition, partly logistics and partly economics — but the pieces (qualification time, continental calendars, player rest, the value of rarity) all point the same way, which is why the four-year cycle has survived a century of change.

In practice

Think through a single player's career arc. He breaks through at 19, plays his first qualifying campaign across two years, reaches his first World Cup at 21. If he lasts into his early thirties, he gets maybe three tournaments — four is legendary territory. Every match of every qualifying campaign carries the weight of that scarcity, which is why a group-stage draw in October can feel like a cup final.

Common misunderstandings

  • The four-year rule is written into football's laws. It is FIFA's calendar and tradition, not a law — the gap has simply never been changed, and the cancelled wartime editions are the only breaks.
  • A biennial World Cup was ruled out because of tradition alone. The objections were practical too: player workload, clashes with continental tournaments and leagues, and the risk of diluting the tournament's value.

Quick questions

When is the next World Cup?

2026, hosted by the United States, Canada and Mexico — the first edition with 48 teams. After that the cycle continues in 2030, with the centenary edition spanning multiple continents.

Has the World Cup ever been held more or less often than every four years?

No — 1930 to 1938 ran every four years (skipping 1942 due to the war), and from 1950 onward the pattern has been unbroken.
