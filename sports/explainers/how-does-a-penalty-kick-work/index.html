Football laws

How does a penalty kick work?

The short answer

A penalty is awarded for a direct-free-kick offence committed by a defender inside their own penalty area. The ball is placed on the penalty spot, the keeper must stay on his line until the ball is kicked — at least one foot on or behind it — and everyone else must be outside the arc and ten yards away. The kicker cannot touch the ball twice.

A penalty is not a special rule so much as a direct free kick moved into the most valuable place on the pitch. Any offence that would be a direct free kick — tripping, pushing, holding, handball — becomes a penalty if the offender is inside his own penalty area when he commits it. The area, not the player's position, is what matters: a defender who fouls just outside the box gives away a free kick, and the same foul a yard further in gives away a penalty.

At the moment of the kick the geometry is strict. The ball sits on the penalty spot, twelve yards from goal. The goalkeeper must remain on his line between the posts until the ball is kicked — at least part of one foot touching, on or behind that line — and he may move along the line, jump and wave his arms, but he may not step forward early. Every other player must be outside the penalty area, outside the penalty arc, and at least ten yards from the ball, which is exactly what the arc is drawn for.

The kicker's obligations are equally fixed. He must kick the ball forward and cannot touch it a second time before someone else does — so a penalty that rebounds off the post or the keeper cannot be tapped in by the same player. Unlike a shootout, though, the rebound in open play is live: if the ball comes back off the woodwork or the keeper, anyone else may follow up and score. That single difference explains why shootouts feel so much colder than penalties during a match.

Encroachment is the most common technical breach, and it usually comes from the attacking team rushing the box. If an attacker enters early and the penalty is scored, it is retaken; if it is missed, the defending team gets an indirect free kick. A goalkeeper who comes off his line early and saves faces a retake too, and repeated encroachment is punished with a caution. Video review checks exactly this, which is why big penalties now come with a long pause before the run-up.

In practice

A defender clips a winger just inside the box and the referee points to the spot. The keeper bounces on his line, the striker stutters in his run-up and waits, then rolls the ball the wrong side of him. Had he struck it against the post and followed up himself, the goal would have been disallowed — but a midfielder arriving late to knock in the rebound would have been perfectly legal.

Common misunderstandings

  • The goalkeeper must keep both feet on the line until the ball is kicked. At least part of one foot must be on, on or behind the line. He can move along it, jump and wave his arms — he just cannot step forward early.
  • The penalty taker can score from his own rebound. No — he cannot touch the ball twice. A team-mate following up can, which is why players sprint into the box as the kick is taken.
  • The penalty arc is just decoration. It marks the ten-yard exclusion zone around the penalty spot. Players must be outside both the area and the arc when the kick is taken.

Quick questions

Can a penalty be taken by anyone?

Any player on the field of play, including the goalkeeper. He must be identified to the referee, and once the ball is kicked the taking is complete.

What happens if the keeper saves it?

In open play the ball stays live and anyone can follow up the rebound — except the original kicker, who cannot touch it twice. In a shootout, the kick is over the moment it is saved.
