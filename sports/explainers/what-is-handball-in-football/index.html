Football laws

What counts as handball in football?

The short answer

A handball is given when a player touches the ball with the hand or arm in a way the law treats as an offence — most clearly when the touch is deliberate, or when the arm makes the body unnaturally bigger or reaches above shoulder height. The arm starts at the bottom of the armpit; the shoulder is not part of it.

The law has two ideas inside it. The first is deliberate touching — the old-school idea of a player clearly handling the ball. The second is arm position: if a player's arm is extended away from the body or raised overhead, it has made the body unnaturally bigger, and contact is usually punished even if the player did not mean it. Between those two ideas sits almost every handball argument you have ever heard.

IFAB has adjusted this wording repeatedly over the years, which is why the rule seems to change every season. The constant is the refereeing logic: arms by the side and close to the body are normally fine; arms out to block, arms above shoulder level, or a clear movement of the hand towards the ball are normally punished. A deflection off a player's own body onto an arm close to the torso is usually not an offence.

Location changes the punishment, not the offence. A handball by an outfield defender inside the penalty area is a penalty kick; the same offence outside the box is a direct free kick. There is also a separate rule for scorers: if the ball goes into the goal off a player's hand or arm — even accidentally — the goal is disallowed for that player, and the ball is treated as a handball offence. A goal cannot be scored directly off a hand or arm, by anyone, in either direction.

Video review makes the rule visible every weekend. Referees are expected to judge arm position and movement with the benefit of replays, and VAR can recommend a review for penalty-area handballs in the build-up to goals. The rule will keep evolving — it is the law IFAB edits most often — but the core test has been stable: deliberate touch, or an arm making the body unnaturally bigger.

In practice

Two identical crosses, two different outcomes. In the first, a defender jumps with his arms pinned to his sides and the ball strikes his forearm — almost never given. In the second, he jumps with his arms raised above his head for balance and the ball hits the same forearm — almost always given, because the arm is beyond shoulder height. Same player, same touch, different verdict: the arm's position is doing the work, not intent.

Common misunderstandings

  • Any contact between ball and arm is handball. No — contact alone decides nothing. The law asks whether the touch was deliberate or the arm made the body unnaturally bigger, and an arm tight to the body is usually ignored.
  • Handball is decided purely by intent. Intent matters, but arm position can be enough on its own — a raised or extended arm is penalised whether or not the player meant it.
  • The scorer rule only applies to attackers going forward. A goal can never be scored directly off a hand or arm at either end, and an attacking handball in the build-up can also wipe out a goal through video review.

Quick questions

Is handball in the penalty area always a penalty?

A handball offence by a defending player inside their own penalty area results in a penalty kick. If the defending team committed no offence recognised by the law — for example an arm tight to the body with no movement — there is no handball and no penalty.

Why does the handball rule seem to change so often?

Because IFAB keeps refining the boundary between deliberate and accidental. It is the law most frequently edited in the entire rulebook, and each rewrite is an attempt to make the threshold clearer for referees.
