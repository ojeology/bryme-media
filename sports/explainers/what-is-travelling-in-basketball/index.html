Basketball rules

What is travelling in basketball?

The short answer

Travelling is moving illegally without dribbling — taking more steps than the rules allow while holding the ball. In simple terms a player may take two steps after gathering the ball, and a player holding the ball in the post may not move his pivot foot before releasing it.

The rule exists to stop a player from walking with the ball, and the whole law turns on the pivot foot. When a player catches the ball with both feet on the floor, either foot may become the pivot. Once he lifts one, the other is established as the pivot, and it must stay in place until he dribbles, passes or shoots. Move it and the whistle goes.

On the move, the standard is two steps. A player gathering the ball while running or after a dribble may take two steps before he must pass, shoot or dribble again — and both the NBA and FIBA now recognise a 'gather step', the step taken while the player is still securing the ball, which does not count against the two. This is why a driving player appears to take three steps legally: the gather, then two.

The common infractions follow from that. Catching the ball and taking three steps is travelling. Sliding the pivot foot along the floor — the infamous 'shuffle' in the post — is travelling. Falling to the floor while holding the ball and then sliding is travelling. And lifting the pivot foot and putting it back down before releasing the ball is travelling, which is why post players are so careful to keep one foot nailed to the floor until they jump.

The punishment is simply loss of possession: it is a violation rather than a personal foul, so no free throws and no foul count. Officials judge travelling more loosely at speed than in the post, because a player moving at full pace is harder to read — and because the modern game's step-back and euro-step moves sit deliberately close to the line. Footwork is a skill precisely because that line exists.

In practice

A forward catches the ball on the wing, jabs right, then drives past his defender with a long gather and two long strides to the rim — legal, because the gather step is not counted and he took two after it. Now take the same player in the post: he establishes his right foot as the pivot, then slides it six inches to get a better angle before shooting. That is travelling, even though he moved far less.

Common misunderstandings

  • You can never take more than two steps, ever. You may take two steps after the gather, and the NBA and FIBA both recognise a gather step that is not counted against the two.
  • Travelling is a foul. It is a violation — the offence loses the ball, but no free throws are awarded and no foul is recorded against anyone.
  • Sliding your pivot foot is fine if you keep it on the floor. Dragging or sliding the pivot foot is travelling. It must stay in place until the ball is released.

Quick questions

What is a gather step?

The step a player takes while still securing the ball. Both the NBA and FIBA allow it, so a driving player effectively gets the gather plus two steps.

Can a player fall to the floor with the ball?

He may not then slide or roll to gain an advantage. Falling is not itself travelling, but moving along the floor while holding the ball is.
