What is the Club World Cup?

The short answer

The Club World Cup is FIFA's global club tournament, contested by the champion clubs of each continental confederation — Europe's Champions League winners, South America's Copa Libertadores winners and the rest. It has existed in various forms since 1960 and expanded to 32 teams for a new edition in 2025.

The idea is older than most fans realise. The Intercontinental Cup, played from 1960, matched Europe's champion against South America's champion over one or two legs; FIFA absorbed it into a broader Club World Championship in 2000 and settled on the annual Club World Cup format in 2005. Its status has always been uneven — taken extremely seriously in South America, and often treated as an obligation by European clubs with a fixture list already at capacity.

Who plays is decided by winning your continent. The Champions League winners take Europe's place, the Copa Libertadores winners take South America's, and the champions of Africa, Asia, North America and Oceania fill the remaining berths, with a place for the host nation's champion in the older format. That structure is what made the competition interesting: it is one of very few places where the best club in Europe meets the best club in Africa or Asia in a competitive match.

The 2025 edition changed the scale entirely — 32 clubs, played over roughly a month in the summer, with the previous annual tournament replaced by a quadrennial event in the same slot the World Cup occupies for national teams. Qualification for the expanded version is based on winning continental titles across a four-year cycle plus a ranking pathway, which means a club's performances over several seasons feed into whether it is invited.

The controversy has followed the ambition. Expanding the tournament added fixtures to an already crowded calendar, and players' unions and leagues raised workload objections; it also redistributes a large prize pool towards the clubs that are already richest. Supporters of the expansion argue it finally gives club football a genuine world championship with real stakes, rather than a short December sideshow.

In practice

A club wins the Champions League in May, then travels to the Club World Cup expecting a relaxed week. Instead it faces the Copa Libertadores winners in front of a full stadium of supporters who have waited decades for the fixture — a match that is a friendly in name for one side and the biggest game in the club's history for the other. That imbalance of meaning is what the expanded format was designed to fix.

Common misunderstandings

  • The Club World Cup and the Intercontinental Cup are unrelated. The Club World Cup grew directly out of the Intercontinental Cup, which matched Europe against South America from 1960 and is recognised as its predecessor.
  • Any club can qualify. Entry comes from winning a continental competition or through a ranking pathway; it is not open to all clubs.
  • It is a friendly tournament. It is a FIFA-organised official competition with a trophy, prize money and, under the expanded format, far greater scale.

Quick questions

How often is the Club World Cup played?

The older format was annual. The expanded 32-team version is designed as a quadrennial summer tournament, with the first of the new era staged in 2025.

Who has won it most often?

European clubs have dominated the modern era, with South American clubs — particularly from Brazil and Argentina — historically the most serious challengers.
