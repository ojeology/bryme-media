Football laws

What does offside mean in football?

The short answer

A player is offside when they are in the opponent's half, beyond the second-last defender, and then get involved in active play at the moment a teammate plays the ball. Standing in an offside position is not an offence by itself — only the involvement turns it into one. The punishment is an indirect free kick to the other team, taken from where the offending player was.

The law has three parts, and all three have to be true at the same instant. First, the player must be in the opponents' half. Second, they must be nearer to the goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent. Third, they must be involved in active play — receiving the pass, challenging for it, blocking a defender, or gaining an advantage from a rebound.

So how does a player actually become offside? He moves into a position where his head, body or feet — hands and arms do not count — are beyond the second-last opponent while the ball is still in the opponent's half, and then a teammate plays the ball forward to him. The 'second-last opponent' is usually the last outfield defender, because the goalkeeper is typically the last one. Being level with that defender is onside: the old rule that you had to be clearly behind him was scrapped in 1990, and today a player level with the line is playing on.

The instant that matters is the moment the ball is touched or played by a teammate — not the moment the receiver gets it. This is why offside is a timing law rather than a positioning law. A striker can start his run a fraction early and be flagged, or wait a fraction longer from exactly the same spot and be clean through. The assistant referee is watching the line at the moment of contact on the ball, which is the single hardest judgement in football.

Getting involved is what converts a position into an offence. The law lists three ways: interfering with play (touching or playing the ball), interfering with an opponent (blocking a defender's line of vision or movement, or challenging him for the ball), and gaining an advantage (collecting the ball after it rebounds off the woodwork, a defender or the goalkeeper). A player standing in an offside position doing none of those three things has committed no offence at all — which is why you see attackers camped behind the last defender with the flag staying down, then sprint back to become active only once the ball is played to someone else.

What happens when offside is given? The referee stops play and awards an indirect free kick to the opposing team, taken from the place where the offending player was at the moment the ball was played — including inside his own half, if that is where he had wandered. The ball must be touched by a second player before a goal can be scored from it, and there is no card: offside is not misconduct, so nobody is booked for being offside alone. A player can, however, be cautioned for deliberately delaying the restart or for leaving the field to engineer an offside trap.

Two technical points settle most arguments. First, a deliberate play by a defender resets offside — if a defender controls the ball and passes or clears it, an attacker in front of him is onside. But a deflection, a block or a save does not reset it: the attacker who was offside when the shot was taken is still offside if the ball rebounds to him. Second, there is no offside offence from a goal kick, a corner kick or a throw-in.

Video review changed how the closest calls are made. In competitions using VAR, the attacking phase is checked with calibrated offside lines: attackers beyond the line by a few centimetres are flagged, and the assistant referees are instructed to keep the flag down on marginal decisions so a goal is not wrongly disallowed and can be awarded on review. The law itself has not changed — only the precision with which the moment is judged.

In practice

Picture a through-ball played toward a striker making a diagonal run between centre-back and full-back. The assistant referee watches the line at the instant of the pass: if the striker's shoulder or knee is beyond the centre-back when the ball leaves the passer's foot, and he then touches the ball, the flag goes up. The referee stops play and awards an indirect free kick from where the striker was standing when the pass was played — not where he touched the ball. If the same run starts half a stride later, everything about the move is legal.

Common misunderstandings

  • Offside means being ahead of the last defender. It is the second-last defender — usually the goalkeeper is the last, so the outfield line is what matters. And being level with that defender is onside.
  • You can be offside from a corner, throw-in or goal kick. You cannot. All three are explicit exceptions in the law — there is no offside offence directly from a restart where the ball is delivered into play from a dead-ball situation like these.
  • If any part of the body is past the line, it is offside. Arms and hands are excluded entirely, and the player still has to be involved in active play before anything is given — being in an offside position on its own is not an offence.
  • You can be offside in your own half. No. The offence can only happen in the opponents' half, which is why the halfway line appears in every offside graphic.
  • Offside is a foul, so the player gets a yellow card. Offside is not misconduct. It is punished with an indirect free kick only, and nobody is cautioned for it — a card comes only for things like delaying the restart.
  • If the ball touches a defender, the attacker is onside. Only a deliberate play by the defender resets offside. A deflection, a block or a goalkeeper's save does not — the attacker who was offside at the moment of the pass stays offside if the rebound reaches him.

Quick questions

What happens when a player is caught offside?

The referee stops play and awards an indirect free kick to the opposing team, taken from where the offending player was when the ball was played. Nobody is cautioned — offside is not a card offence.

Where is the free kick taken from?

From the position of the offside player at the moment the ball was played by his teammate — which can be in the opponents' half or, if he had strayed back, in his own.

Can a player be offside if he does not touch the ball?

Yes. Interfering with an opponent — blocking a defender's line of vision or movement — or gaining an advantage from a rebound counts, even if he never touches the ball.

Why is the flag sometimes delayed?

In competitions with VAR, assistant referees keep the flag down on marginal decisions so a legitimate goal is not wrongly disallowed; the review then confirms whether the player was offside.
