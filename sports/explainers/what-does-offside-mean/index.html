Football laws

What does offside mean in football?

The short answer

A player is offside when they are in the opponent's half, beyond the second-last defender, and then get involved in active play at the moment a teammate plays the ball. Standing in an offside position is not an offence by itself — only the involvement turns it into one.

The law has three parts, and all three have to be true at the same instant. First, the player must be in the opponents' half. Second, they must be nearer to the goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent. Third, they must be involved in active play — receiving the pass, challenging for it, blocking a defender, or gaining an advantage from a rebound.

Because the decision is frozen at the moment the ball is played by a teammate, a striker can sprint past the defence a second later and still be onside. The reverse is the famous heartbreak: a perfectly timed run flagged because the passer waited a fraction too long. This is why offside is described as a timing law, not a positioning law.

The hands and arms do not count for offside — only the head, body and feet. A player who is level with the second-last defender is onside; the old idea of needing to be clearly behind the defender ended in 1990. There is also no offside directly from a throw-in, a corner or a goal kick.

In practice

Picture a through-ball played toward a striker making a diagonal run between centre-back and full-back. The assistant referee watches the line at the instant of the pass: if the striker's shoulder or knee is beyond the centre-back when the ball leaves the passer's foot, the flag goes up when the striker touches the ball. If the same run is started half a stride later, everything about the move stays legal.

Common misunderstandings

  • "Offside means being ahead of the last defender." It is the second-last defender — usually the goalkeeper is the last, so the outfield line is what matters.
  • "You can be offside from a corner or a throw-in." You cannot. Corners, throw-ins and goal kicks are explicit exceptions in the law.
  • "If any part of the body is past the line, it is offside." Arms and hands are excluded, and the player still has to be involved in active play before anything is given.

Quick questions

When exactly is the offside line judged?

At the moment the ball is last played by a teammate. Everything after that — where the attacker runs, where the defender steps — is irrelevant to the position itself.

Who counts as the second-last opponent?

The second-closest opponent to their own goal line at the moment of the pass. Usually that means the last outfield defender, with the goalkeeper behind them.
