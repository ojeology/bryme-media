Match mechanics

How long is a football match — and why 90 minutes?

The short answer

A standard match is two halves of 45 minutes with a 15-minute half-time interval, plus stoppage time at the end of each half. The 90 minutes is convention rather than physics — it was settled in the 19th century and never seriously changed, because the game's rhythm, tactics and stamina are all built around it.

The 90-minute standard emerged from the 1860s and 1870s, when English clubs and the newly formed Football Association settled on two halves and a fixed duration as a compromise between matches that dragged on and matches that decided nothing. Earlier fixtures had been played to arbitrary lengths, and some were even decided by the first goal regardless of time. Two 45-minute halves simply won the argument and became the global norm.

What that means in practice is longer than the clock suggests. Referees add stoppage time at the end of each half for time lost to substitutions, injuries, celebrations, video reviews and time-wasting, and that addition has grown sharply — matches now routinely pass 100 minutes in total. Add the half-time interval and a typical top-level match occupies close to two hours of a viewer's evening, even though the ball is only in play for about 55 to 60 minutes of it.

Every other duration in the sport is calibrated against that baseline. Youth matches are shorter because the players are; extra time is two further periods of fifteen minutes for knockout ties that must produce a winner; and the half-time interval is fixed at fifteen minutes by the law, with any longer break requiring the referee's authorisation and usually being reported afterwards.

The one thing the laws will not let a competition do casually is change the 90 minutes themselves. Trials of shorter matches with a stopped clock have been discussed — rugby and basketball both stop the clock for dead time — but the clock running continuously is baked into football's identity, and the recent reforms have gone the other way: counting lost time more strictly rather than shortening the match.

In practice

A Saturday afternoon kick-off that starts at 3pm usually ends around 4:50pm. Ninety minutes of clock, plus one minute of added time in the first half, eight in the second, fifteen minutes of half-time, and the four or five minutes the teams take to walk off and return. The ball, for all that, is in play for barely an hour.

Common misunderstandings

  • A football match is exactly 90 minutes long. That is the regulation playing time. Stoppage time at the end of each half is added on top, and modern matches frequently exceed 100 minutes in total.
  • Half-time is as long as the referee wants. The interval is fifteen minutes by law. Anything longer has to be authorised and is normally reported.
  • Youth and amateur matches use the same 90 minutes. Shorter halves are standard for younger age groups and many amateur competitions; the 90-minute format is the professional adult norm.

Quick questions

Why not stop the clock like other sports?

Continuous time is part of football's tradition and its tactical texture — managing the clock is a skill. Trials of a stopped clock have been discussed but not adopted at the top of the game.

How long is the ball actually in play?

Typically around 55 to 60 minutes of a 90-minute match, which is the statistic driving the clampdown on time-wasting and the longer added times.
