Football language

What does a captain actually do?

The short answer

The captain is the team's on-field representative: he takes part in the coin toss, is the player the referee addresses about conduct, and is expected to manage his team-mates' behaviour. The armband carries no extra authority in the laws — the referee does not have to speak to him, and he cannot appeal decisions on anyone else's behalf.

The laws give the captain almost no formal power, which surprises most people. He has no right to demand an explanation, no right to see a card overturned, and no special status when he commits an offence — a captain who fouls is punished exactly like anyone else. What he does have is a responsibility: the referee will often use him to calm a situation, and persistent misbehaviour by his team-mates reflects on him.

His visible duties are few but symbolic. He takes part in the coin toss before kick-off and before a penalty shootout, choosing ends or taking the kick-off, and later choosing whether his team shoots first. He wears the armband, which is why the role is described as an honour rather than a job — and why the armband is passed on so conspicuously when a captain is substituted.

The real work is invisible. A captain organises the defensive line, relays instructions from the bench, settles a young player who has just made a mistake, and decides when to complain — and when to pull his team-mates away from a referee. This is why the armband usually goes to a player who is respected rather than the most talented one, and why some of the game's most influential captains have been defenders and holding midfielders rather than star forwards.

Clubs handle the role differently. Some appoint a permanent captain and a vice-captain; others rotate it; a few have a council of senior players with one wearing the armband. At international level the armband is often a statement of standing in the squad — the player who will lead the team through a tournament — and removing it from someone is one of the most consequential decisions a manager can make.

In practice

A defender is sent off and his team-mates surround the referee. The captain arrives, takes the player by the arm and walks him towards the touchline while saying something to the referee that de-escalates rather than inflames. Nothing in the laws required him to do that — it is the job the armband implies, and doing it well is the difference between finishing with ten men and finishing with nine.

Common misunderstandings

  • Captains can ask the referee to explain or reverse decisions. They have no formal right to do so. Referees may choose to speak to the captain, but nothing in the laws compels it.
  • A captain cannot be booked or sent off like other players. He is subject to exactly the same laws. A captain who dissents can be cautioned, and a captain can be dismissed.
  • The captain is always the best player. Clubs usually pick a leader — often a defender or midfielder — because the role is about organising and representing rather than scoring.

Quick questions

What happens to the armband if the captain is substituted?

He hands it to another player — usually the vice-captain. There is no requirement to do so, but convention is universal.

Can a goalkeeper be captain?

Yes. Goalkeepers are common captains because they see the whole pitch and organise the defence in front of them.
