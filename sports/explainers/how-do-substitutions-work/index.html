Match mechanics

How do substitutions work?

The short answer

Most competitions allow five substitutes from a matchday squad of a larger bench, made in up to three separate stoppages (plus half-time). A substituted player cannot return, the substitute must enter at the halfway line during a stoppage, and the ball must be out of play.

The modern standard is five substitutions from nine named on the bench, introduced permanently in 2020 and retained afterwards. Those five have to be used within a maximum of three substitution windows, with half-time counting separately — so a manager cannot make five changes at five different stoppages. Competitions set their own exact numbers, so lower leagues and older competitions still run on three.

The mechanics are strict. The ball must be out of play, the referee must be notified and signal, the player leaving must go off at the nearest point on the boundary (or the halfway line, if the referee allows), and the incoming player cannot enter until the outgoing one has left the field. Coming on before your teammate leaves the pitch is a bookable offence, and it happens more often than you would expect in the chaos of a late change.

There are three specialist cases. A goalkeeper can be substituted like anyone else, but a team must always have a keeper — if no keeper remains, an outfield player changes shirt and takes the gloves. A substitute who comes on cannot be substituted off again (except in some youth and amateur competitions with return substitutions). And a player substituted before kick-off is simply replaced on the team sheet, with no substitution counted.

The fifth substitute changed tactics more than any law change of the last decade. Five changes let a manager field an entirely new front three for the last half-hour, which favours squads with depth — the reason 'the bench won that game' became a real analysis rather than a cliché. It also produced the tactical foul-by-committee approach: fresh legs every fifteen minutes to press a tired opponent.

In practice

A manager makes a double change in the 60th minute (window one), a triple change in the 78th (window two), and uses the interval for nothing at all. That is five substitutions across three windows — legal. Make the same five in five separate stoppages and the fourth and fifth are refused by the fourth official, however loudly the bench protests.

Common misunderstandings

  • You can make five substitutions at any five stoppages. Five changes must fit into at most three windows, with half-time separate. The stoppage count is the limit people forget.
  • A substituted player can come back on if someone gets injured. No — once substituted, a player's involvement in the match is over, with narrow exceptions only in youth and amateur football.
  • Substitutions can be made while the ball is in play. The ball must be out of play and the referee must signal before the change is completed.

Quick questions

How many substitutes are named on the bench?

It varies by competition; the Premier League names nine, and five of those can be used. Older competitions and some leagues still name seven and allow three.

Can a substitute take a penalty in a shootout?

Yes — any player on the field at the final whistle is eligible, including substitutes who came on during the match.
