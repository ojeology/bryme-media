Match mechanics

What does the coin toss decide?

The short answer

Before kick-off the referee tosses a coin and the winning captain chooses which goal to attack in the first half or takes the kick-off. The other team gets whichever he does not pick. If the match goes to penalties, a second toss decides who kicks first and which end the shootout is taken at.

The pre-match toss decides two things. The captain who wins it chooses either which end his team attacks in the first half, or the right to take the kick-off; the losing captain takes whatever is left. The teams swap ends at half-time, so the choice is really about which half a team wants the wind, the sun or the slope behind them — a decision that can matter enormously in an exposed stadium and not at all in a modern bowl.

The second toss, before a penalty shootout, is far more consequential. The winning captain chooses whether his team kicks first or second, and the referee may also decide which goal is used — often the one in front of the noisier end, to give the shootout atmosphere, with both teams shooting at it. Kicking first means setting the pace; kicking second means always knowing exactly what you need. Research and convention both suggest kicking first is a slight advantage, because the chasing team faces repeated must-score moments.

The other side of the coin is the kick-off itself. The team that kicks off starts with the ball but does not get to choose ends; the other team chooses ends and receives the ball for the second half. A kick-off must be taken forwards, everyone must be in their own half, and the ball is in play once it is kicked and clearly moves — and a goal can in principle be scored directly from it, though nobody has ever done it from the centre circle in a top-level match.

There is a tactical layer people miss: the choice of ends is often about the second half, not the first. A team that expects to be defending a lead late, or attacking towards its own supporters in the closing minutes, will pick its end accordingly. Captains are coached on this, and some consult the bench before the toss rather than making the obvious choice.

In practice

A captain wins the toss in a stadium with a strong wind behind one goal and chooses to attack that end in the second half, when his team's fitter bench and tiring opponents should tell. At 1-1 in the 88th minute, the wind is at his forwards' backs for a final assault. Ninety minutes later the same captain wins the shootout toss and elects to kick first — a decision that will be called inspired or foolish depending on how the first five kicks go.

Common misunderstandings

  • The toss decides who gets the ball in both halves. It decides ends or the kick-off. The team that does not kick off takes the ball for the second half, so both teams get one restart.
  • The shootout toss only decides who kicks first. It can also decide which goal the shootout is taken at, and the referee may select the end to put the shootout in front of the louder support.
  • You cannot score directly from a kick-off. The laws allow it — the ball is in play once kicked and clearly moving. It is simply never attempted from the centre circle.

Quick questions

Who makes the choice at the toss?

The captain of the team that wins it. He may pick which end to attack in the first half, or take the kick-off — not both.

Is kicking first in a shootout an advantage?

Most analysis suggests a modest edge to going first, because it forces the opponent into repeated must-score situations — though many managers and players prefer second.
