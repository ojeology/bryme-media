Football laws

When is a goal actually a goal?

The short answer

A goal counts when the whole of the ball crosses the whole of the goal line, between the posts and under the crossbar — and the scoring team committed no offence in the build-up. Offside, handball by the scorer, a foul by an attacker or the ball earlier going out of play can all wipe a goal out after it has been celebrated.

The physical test is the strictest thing in the sport: the entire ball must be past the entire goal line. In practice that means millimetre calls are invisible to the naked eye — a ball can straddle the line with a crescent visible to the camera and still be no goal, or be fractionally over and billions of viewers will swear it was not. That ambiguity is exactly why goal-line technology exists.

Goal-line technology, approved by IFAB in 2012 and used by the Premier League since 2013-14, puts a vibration and a visual signal on the referee's watch within a second of the ball crossing the line. It was introduced after the notorious 2010 World Cup case where a shot rebounded off the crossbar, clearly crossed the line, and was not given — the most famous goal that never was. The technology's sole job is the line: it says nothing about offside, fouls or anything else.

The rest of the decision belongs to the referee and video review. VAR can check the build-up to any goal for offside, handball by the scorer, a foul by the attacking team, or the ball going out of play before the goal. Because a goal is a match-defining moment, it is one of the two reviewable 'stoppage' categories with the widest checks — and this is why a goal six passes and forty seconds after a fractional handball can still be disallowed.

Two supporting laws complete the picture. A goal cannot be scored directly off a hand or arm by anyone — the scorer's version of the handball rule — and it cannot be scored directly from an indirect free kick, a throw-in, a goal kick or a kick-off. Everything else — distance, keeper's touch, a deflection off a defender — is a legal goal, which is why deflected winners count exactly the same as thirty-yard screamers in the record books.

In practice

A striker lashes the ball goalwards, the goalkeeper spills it across the line, and the stadium erupts. Three things then get checked off-screen in seconds: did the whole ball cross the whole line (goal-line technology, no debate); was anyone offside in the build-up (VAR, calibrated lines); and did the scorer handle the ball (VAR, slow motion). When all three clear, the goal stands and the technology that made it possible vanishes from the story entirely.

Common misunderstandings

  • It is a goal if the ball touches or rolls along the line. Only when the whole of the ball crosses the whole of the line. A ball on the line is still in play.
  • Goal-line technology also checks offsides and fouls. It checks one thing only: whether the ball crossed the line. Offside and build-up fouls are VAR's department, using different cameras and different rules.

Quick questions

Why do some leagues not have goal-line technology?

Cost. The systems require cameras, calibration and licensing fees per stadium, and smaller leagues and lower divisions often cannot justify the expense, leaving decisions to the referee's eyes.

Can VAR award a goal the referee missed?

Yes for the specific reviewable situations. The unusual case is goal-line calls: those are resolved by goal-line technology, not VAR, because the technology gives a definitive answer instantly.
