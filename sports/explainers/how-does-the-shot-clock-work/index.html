Basketball rules

How does the shot clock work in basketball?

The short answer

The shot clock gives the attacking team a limited time to attempt a shot — 24 seconds in the NBA and FIBA, 30 in college basketball. It resets when the ball hits the rim, when possession changes, or after certain fouls, and an offensive rebound resets it to 14 seconds rather than the full 24.

The shot clock exists to stop teams simply holding the ball. Before it was introduced in 1954, a leading team could stall effectively forever, and games became so slow that the rules had to change. The clock starts when a team gains possession and runs down to zero unless the ball is released and touches the rim — a shot that airballs or misses everything completely is still a violation unless it hits the rim, which is the detail most newcomers miss.

The length depends on the competition: 24 seconds in the NBA and under FIBA rules, and 30 seconds in men's and women's college basketball. The same eight-second rule applies in all of them for advancing the ball past the halfway line — take longer and it is a backcourt violation and a turnover. Together, the two clocks are what give basketball its pace: you have eight seconds to get up the floor and roughly twenty-four to get a shot off.

Resets are where the rule gets interesting. A shot that hits the rim restarts the clock; a missed shot that does not hit anything is a violation. If the offence grabs its own rebound, the clock resets to 14 seconds rather than the full 24 — a change introduced so second-chance opportunities do not restart an entire possession. And when there are fewer than 14 seconds left on the clock, the reset does not extend it.

Violations are punished simply: the shot-clock buzzer sounds, the offence loses the ball, and the other team inbounds it from the sideline. There is no free throw and no penalty beyond the turnover, but losing a possession in a close game is punishment enough. Officials watch the clock, an operator runs it, and the reset after a deflection or a jump ball is one of the most argued-about seconds in the sport — which is why modern arenas display it on the backboard as well as above it.

In practice

A team grabs an offensive rebound with nine seconds left on the shot clock. Under the old rules the clock would have gone back to a full 24, giving them a completely fresh possession; today it stays at nine, because the reset to 14 seconds never extends a clock that is already shorter. The result is a hurried shot, which is exactly the pressure the rule was designed to create.

Common misunderstandings

  • Any shot beats the shot clock. The ball must be released AND hit the rim. An airball is a violation even if it left the shooter's hand in time.
  • The shot clock always resets to 24. An offensive rebound resets it to 14 seconds in the NBA and under FIBA rules — and never extends a clock that is already shorter.
  • The shot clock is the same everywhere. It is 24 seconds in the NBA and FIBA, and 30 seconds in college basketball.

Quick questions

How long is the shot clock?

24 seconds in the NBA and under FIBA rules; 30 seconds in college basketball. Teams must also advance the ball past half court within eight seconds.

What happens if the shot clock expires?

It is a violation, not a foul. The offence loses possession and the other team inbounds the ball from the sideline.
