Football laws

How do throw-ins, goal kicks and corners work?

The short answer

All three are ways of putting the ball back into play after it goes out, and each has its own geometry: a throw-in is taken with both hands from behind the head at the point where the ball crossed the touchline; a goal kick is taken from inside the six-yard box after the attackers last touched it out over the goal line; a corner is taken from the corner arc after the defenders last touched it out.

The direction of the restart is decided by who touched the ball last. Over the touchline, the team that did not touch it last takes the throw-in. Over the goal line, it depends on who got the final touch: attackers last means a goal kick to the defenders, defenders last means a corner to the attackers. That is the whole logic — one question, and the restart follows.

Throw-ins have the strictest technique in the game. At the moment of release the thrower must face the pitch, have part of each foot on or behind the touchline, use both hands, and deliver the ball from behind and over his head. A foul throw — one foot in the air, a sideways delivery, a spin — hands possession to the other team. There is also no offside from a throw-in, so a team can launch it to a player standing behind the entire defence.

Goal kicks and corners are the mirror image of each other. A goal kick is taken from anywhere inside the six-yard box, and the ball is not in play until it has left the penalty area — if it is touched before that, the kick is retaken. Opponents must be outside the penalty area when it is taken. A corner is taken from inside the corner arc, the corner flag cannot be moved, opponents must be ten yards away until the ball is in play, and a goal can be scored directly from it — the famous Olympic goal, rare but legal.

These restarts are where the smallest technical details decide matches. A throw-in taken from the wrong spot is given to the opposition; a goal kick played short inside the box must be retaken; a corner taken from a ball that is moving or slightly outside the arc invites a dispute. Referees enforce them inconsistently, but the law is precise — which is why set-piece coaches spend hours on throw-ins in particular.

In practice

A long throw sails into the six-yard box and is flicked on at the near post for a goal. Entirely legal: there is no offside from a throw-in, so the attacker who flicked it on could stand wherever he liked. Now imagine the same delivery from a corner — the attacker would have had to be onside at the moment the ball was played, because corners, unlike throw-ins, do not exempt the attacking team from the offside law in the same way.

Common misunderstandings

  • You can be offside from a throw-in. No — throw-ins, corners and goal kicks are the three restarts from which there is no offside offence.
  • A goal kick is live as soon as it is kicked. The ball is not in play until it has clearly left the penalty area. If anyone touches it before that, the kick is retaken.
  • You cannot score directly from a corner. You can. The Olympic goal — straight in from a corner without a second touch — is rare but entirely legal.

Quick questions

Can a goalkeeper score directly from a goal kick?

Yes — if it goes into the opposition goal without touching anyone, it counts. It cannot count as an own goal against his own team directly from a goal kick; that would be a corner.

Why do referees rarely punish foul throws?

They do at the top level, but the margin is small and the throw is quick. Lawfully, a foul throw simply gives possession to the other team.
