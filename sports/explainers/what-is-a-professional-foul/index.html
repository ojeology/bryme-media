Football laws

What is a professional foul?

The short answer

A professional foul is a deliberate offence committed to stop a dangerous attack — usually hauling down a player through on goal. It is punished with a red card when it denies an obvious goal-scoring opportunity, unless the offender was genuinely trying to play the ball inside the penalty area, in which case the punishment is a penalty and a yellow card.

The concept is a calculation, not a specific action. A defender decides that conceding a free kick and taking a card is a better outcome than conceding a certain goal, so he commits the offence deliberately. It is cynical but rational, which is why the law has been rewritten repeatedly to make the calculation less attractive — the whole history of this rule is an attempt to make the punishment roughly match the crime.

The modern test turns on whether an obvious goal-scoring opportunity was denied. The referee weighs four things: the distance to goal, the direction of play, the likelihood of the attacker keeping or gaining control, and the position and number of defenders. If the offence denied an obvious chance, the offender is sent off — that is the 'last man' red card fans know well. It applies to handball as well as to fouls: a deliberate handball that stops a certain goal is a professional foul too.

The penalty-area exception is the part that surprises people. If the offence happens inside the penalty area and the defender was making a genuine attempt to play the ball, the punishment is a penalty kick plus a yellow card rather than a red — the so-called double jeopardy rule, changed around 2016. The logic is that a penalty is already a very high-value punishment, and sending the player off as well meant one mistake ended the match twice over. Holding, pulling, pushing or a handball that stops the ball — anything that is not a genuine attempt to play it — is still a red.

Few decisions in football are as consequential. A professional foul in the tenth minute shapes the next eighty: the opposition plays against ten men, the manager restructures, and the offender serves a suspension. That is why players are coached on where to make the challenge, and why some defenders deliberately foul outside the box rather than inside it — the maths change completely depending on which side of the line it happens.

In practice

A striker breaks clear with only the keeper to beat. The last defender grabs his shirt and pulls him down thirty yards out: obvious goal-scoring opportunity denied, straight red card. Now imagine the same incident inside the penalty area, with the defender genuinely lunging for the ball and catching the attacker instead — penalty kick and a yellow card. Same cynicism, same denial, two completely different outcomes determined by eighteen yards of grass.

Common misunderstandings

  • Any last-man foul is a red card. Inside the penalty area a genuine attempt to play the ball that results in a penalty is only a caution. Outside the box, or for holding, pulling or handball, denying an obvious chance is a red.
  • The double jeopardy rule removed red cards for professional fouls entirely. It only softened them for genuine attempts to play the ball inside the penalty area. Deliberate holding, pulling or a handball that stops the ball is still a sending-off.
  • A professional foul only means tripping someone. Handball counts too — a deliberate hand that stops a certain goal is a professional foul and is punished the same way.

Quick questions

What counts as an obvious goal-scoring opportunity?

The referee weighs distance to goal, general direction of play, the likelihood of keeping or gaining control of the ball, and the position and number of defenders.

Is a professional foul punished differently in a shootout?

Yes — during a shootout a player who commits an offence can be cautioned, but the shootout continues with the kicking team's kick recorded as missed if the foul prevented a goal.
