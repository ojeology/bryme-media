Basketball rules

What is the three-second rule?

The short answer

The three-second rule stops an attacking player from standing in the painted area under the basket for more than three seconds while his team has the ball. There is also a defensive version in the NBA, which stops defenders from camping in the lane without guarding anyone.

The offensive rule is the one most people know. While his team has possession, an attacking player may not remain in the painted rectangle — the lane, or 'the key' — for more than three consecutive seconds. He can dip in and out, and the count restarts each time he leaves, but he cannot plant himself next to the rim waiting for a pass. The purpose is to stop a big man from parking under the basket and making the game unplayable for everyone else.

The count only runs while the team has the ball, and it does not start while the player is actively shooting or while the ball is in the air on a shot. This is why offensive players drift in and out of the lane rather than standing in it — they are resetting the count. Referees count visibly, and the violation is called more often than most fans expect precisely because it is so easy to lose track.

The defensive three-second rule exists only in the NBA. It prohibits a defender from staying in the lane for more than three seconds unless he is actively guarding an opponent within arm's length — which stops teams from hiding a weak defender near the rim and clogging the paint. The penalty is a technical foul: one free throw and possession. Under FIBA rules there is no defensive three-second violation, which is one of the reasons international defence looks so different from NBA defence.

The two rules together shape the modern game. Without them, the area under the basket would be permanently occupied by the tallest player on each team, and the sport would collapse into a series of post-ups. With them, the lane has to be vacated and re-entered constantly, which is what creates the cutting, spacing and pick-and-roll movement that defines basketball now.

In practice

A centre sets up camp on the block, five feet from the basket, while his team swings the ball around the perimeter. The referee raises a hand and counts: three seconds, whistle, turnover. Had he stepped out to the free-throw line and back in again each time, he could have stayed involved legally — the rule punishes occupation, not presence.

Common misunderstandings

  • The rule applies to defenders as well as attackers everywhere. Defensive three seconds exists only in the NBA. FIBA and most other competitions have no equivalent.
  • You cannot be in the lane at all on offence. You can be there — you just cannot remain there for more than three consecutive seconds while your team has the ball.
  • The count runs the whole time. It only runs while your team has possession, and it pauses during a shot.

Quick questions

Is defensive three seconds called outside the NBA?

No. It is an NBA rule. FIBA competitions have no defensive three-second violation, which changes how teams protect the rim.

What is the penalty for offensive three seconds?

Loss of possession — it is a violation rather than a foul, so the defence inbounds the ball from the sideline.
