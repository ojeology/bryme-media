Knockout football

What is extra time?

The short answer

Extra time is two additional halves of fifteen minutes played when a knockout match must produce a winner and the score is level after ninety minutes. If it is still level, the tie goes to penalties — and unlike league football, extra time is not played unless the competition requires a winner.

Extra time exists only in matches that cannot end level: cup ties, knockout rounds and tournament finals. Its structure is two fifteen-minute periods with a short break between them, and teams swap ends before the first begins. There is no 'sudden death' in the modern game — both periods are always played in full, because the golden goal and silver goal experiments were both abandoned.

Golden goal ended matches the moment anyone scored, and it produced some famously anticlimactic endings; silver goal allowed a team to win at the end of the first extra period if ahead. UEFA used both in the late 1990s and early 2000s and scrapped them, returning to a full thirty minutes. The law now allows competitions to permit an extra substitution in extra time, which most do — an acknowledgement that thirty more minutes on tired legs changes the maths of the bench.

Tactically, extra time is its own match. Teams who have defended for ninety minutes to reach it often discover that a further thirty is exactly the opposite of what they wanted: the fitter side, and the side with a deeper bench, usually wins. This is why managers talk about 'winning it in ninety' — reaching extra time is not a neutral outcome, it is a shift in who the match favours.

The relationship with penalties is the reason the whole thing feels tense. Every minute of extra time is a minute spent avoiding a shootout, which produces the strange spectacle of two exhausted teams attacking cautiously — neither willing to lose the match outright, both aware that a goal saves them from the lottery.

In practice

A cup final is 1-1 at ninety minutes. Thirty more minutes follow, both teams now onto their fourth and fifth substitutes, legs gone, space appearing where there was none all evening. A substitute — on for a tiring full-back — scores in the 110th minute. If nobody scores, the match is officially recorded as a draw and the tie is decided from twelve yards.

Common misunderstandings

  • Extra time is sudden death. No — both fifteen-minute periods are played in full. Golden goal and silver goal were tried and abandoned.
  • League matches go to extra time when drawn. They do not. Extra time only appears where a competition must produce a winner.
  • You cannot make further substitutions in extra time. Most competitions allow one additional substitution in extra time, even if the team has used all its normal windows.

Quick questions

How long is the break before extra time?

A short interval — usually around five minutes — before teams change ends and the first extra period begins.

Is the result recorded as a draw if it goes to penalties?

Yes. The official match result is a draw; the shootout decides who advances and is recorded separately.
