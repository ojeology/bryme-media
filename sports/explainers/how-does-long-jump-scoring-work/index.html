How does long-jump scoring work?

The short answer

In the long jump, an athlete sprints down a runway, takes off before the foul line and lands in a sand pit. Judges measure the distance from the take-off line to the nearest break in the sand, at right angles to the line. The athlete’s best valid jump normally decides the result; a take-off foul is recorded as a failed trial, not a measured jump.

Long jump combines a fast approach with a controlled take-off and a landing. The athlete accelerates along the runway, then pushes off from one foot before the take-off line and lands in the sand. The approach does not have to end on the board itself: an athlete may take off from behind the line, but that leaves less distance available to add to the mark. The aim is to approach close enough to use the board without crossing its legal limit.

A take-off is a failure if the take-off foot or shoe breaks the vertical plane of the take-off line before the athlete has ceased contact with the board or ground. This is more precise than the old shortcut ‘your toe touched the plasticine’: the current rule concerns the shoe or foot crossing the vertical plane, and video or other technology is encouraged to help judges decide close calls. A foul is shown as a failed trial and does not produce a distance for the standings.

For a valid jump, the mark is measured from the take-off line to the nearest break in the landing area made by any part of the athlete’s body. The measurement is taken at right angles to the take-off line. That means a hand or heel that touches the sand behind the main landing mark can determine the result; judges do not measure to the athlete’s feet if another body mark is closer to the line.

The competition format depends on the event regulations. In the standard World Athletics field-event format when more than eight athletes compete, everyone gets three trials and the eight with the best valid performances receive three additional trials. Championship qualification rounds can use their own rules and are separate from the final. The mark that matters for an athlete’s placing is normally their longest valid jump, not the average of their attempts.

If two athletes have the same best distance, the next-best valid mark is used to separate them; if that is also equal, officials compare the next mark in the series, following the event’s tie-breaking rules. Wind is recorded in the long jump. A tailwind above +2.0m/s can stop a performance from being ratified as a World Record, but it does not turn a valid competition jump into a foul or remove it from the event result.

Reading a result sheet is therefore straightforward once the symbols are understood. A measured distance is a valid trial, an X is a failure, and a dash means the athlete passed. An athlete can win with a single good legal jump even if several later attempts are fouls, because the best valid mark is what counts. The runway and sandpit look simple; the board, the landing mark and the rules make the measurement exact.

In practice

An athlete leaves the board legally and lands feet-first, but their hand touches the sand behind the heel marks as they sit back. The judge measures from the take-off line to the nearest mark—the hand mark—at a right angle to the line. The jump is shorter than it would have been if the athlete had kept their balance and landed beyond that mark.

Common misunderstandings

  • The jump is measured to the athlete’s toes. It is measured to the nearest break in the landing area made by any part of the athlete’s body.
  • A toe on the board is always a foul. The rule asks whether the take-off foot or shoe breaks the vertical plane of the line before the athlete has stopped contacting the board or ground.
  • A strong tailwind makes the jump a foul. Wind affects record ratification and classification, but it does not by itself make an otherwise legal jump a failed trial.

Quick questions

How is a long jump measured?

From the take-off line to the nearest break in the sand made by any part of the athlete’s body, measured at right angles to the line.

How many attempts does a long jumper get?

The standard format for a field of more than eight gives everyone three trials, then the eight best valid marks get three more. Meet regulations can set a different format.

Does a wind-aided long jump count?

The jump can remain a valid competition result, but a tailwind above +2.0m/s prevents World Athletics record ratification under the applicable rules.
