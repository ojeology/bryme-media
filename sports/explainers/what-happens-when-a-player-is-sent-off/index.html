Football laws

What happens when a player is sent off?

The short answer

A sent-off player leaves the field immediately and his team plays the rest of the match a player short — he cannot be replaced. He is also automatically suspended, usually for at least one match, with longer bans for serious offences, and a team that drops below seven players forfeits the game.

A red card arrives in one of two ways: a straight red for a serious offence — violent conduct, a denial of a goal-scoring opportunity, serious foul play, spitting, or abusive language — or two yellow cards in the same match, which add up to one dismissal. The two routes carry the same immediate consequence: the player goes off, his team continues with ten, and the manager has to reorganise on the spot.

The tactical maths change everything. Playing a man down for twenty minutes is survivable; playing most of a match a man short usually decides it, so managers sacrifice an attacker, drop everyone deeper and try to defend set pieces. A red card for the goalkeeper forces a different scramble: either a substitute keeper comes on — if the team still has substitution slots available — or an outfield player pulls on the gloves for the rest of the game.

The suspension lasts longer than the heat of the moment. Every competition writes its own tariffs: a straight red for violent conduct typically brings a longer ban than a professional foul, two yellow cards typically bring a one-match ban, and repeat offences escalate. Yellow cards also accumulate across a season in most leagues, so a player can be suspended without ever being sent off. Clubs can appeal wrongful dismissals, and bans served in one competition sometimes carry over to others.

There are hard floors in the law, too. A team reduced below seven players must abandon the match and forfeits it — the referee has no discretion to continue. And in a penalty shootout, a player sent off cannot take part: the shootout list only includes players eligible at the final whistle, so a red card in extra time can shrink the shootout pool in a way that only becomes obvious when the takers are chosen.

In practice

A centre-back collects a second yellow in the 70th minute for a late tackle. Ten-man club defends a 1-0 lead: the manager removes a winger for a replacement centre-back, the whole team drops ten metres deeper, and every set piece becomes a siege. The centre-back misses the next match automatically, and the club also loses him for the season's remaining matchdays if the accumulation rules bite. One tackle, three consequences — the match, the next fixture, and a reshuffled defence for a month.

Common misunderstandings

  • A sent-off player can be replaced by a substitute. No — a dismissal reduces the team permanently for that match, which is exactly why the punishment is so heavy.
  • Only straight reds carry suspensions. Two yellows in one match also mean a ban, and leagues add suspensions for accumulating yellow cards across a season.

Quick questions

What happens if the goalkeeper is sent off?

If the team still has substitution windows available, a substitute goalkeeper comes on and an outfield player is removed. If no substitution is available, an outfield player finishes the match in goal.

Can a red card be overturned?

Yes — clubs can appeal wrongful dismissals to a disciplinary panel, which can rescind the card and the suspension. Success rate is low, because the panel must find a clear error rather than merely disagree.
