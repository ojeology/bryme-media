Football laws

What do referee signals mean?

The short answer

Referees use a small set of standard signals so decisions are understood across language barriers: a horizontal arm for a direct free kick, one arm straight up for an indirect one, a pointed arm for a goal kick, corner or throw-in, and cards for cautions and dismissals. Assistant flags add the rest — angle tells you which restart, height tells you who gets it.

The lingua franca of football is body language, and it is standardised. A direct free kick is signalled with one arm held out horizontally in the direction of the kick, because the ball can go straight in from it. An indirect free kick is signalled with one arm raised vertically and held there until the ball touches a second player or goes out of play — that vertical arm is the single most useful signal for a fan to know, because it tells you the kick cannot score directly.

Restarts each have their own gesture. A goal kick is a horizontal arm pointing at the goal area; a corner is the same arm pointing at the corner arc; a throw-in is an arm raised at an angle, then swung in the direction of the throw — with the raised arm indicating the thrower's team, which is why the signal looks like a pointing motion after a stoppage. Advantage is two arms swept forward with a shout of 'play on', and a penalty is a single arm pointing at the spot.

Assistant referees complete the picture with the flag. The angle of the flag tells you which restart: up at 45 degrees for a throw-in to the attacking team, down at 45 degrees for one to the defending team, horizontal for a goal kick, and low and pointed at the corner flag for a corner. The height of the flag at an offside tells you where it happened — level with the hip for near, at 45 degrees for the middle, and high for the far side of the pitch. A flag waved in the centre, rather than held, signals a foul the referee did not see.

The referee's cards are the part everyone knows — yellow for a caution, red for a dismissal, two yellows then a red for a second bookable offence. But referees also note who committed the offence before showing anything, and at free kicks they will often hold up the number of the player who must be spoken to. Watch the referee's other hand at a set piece and you can usually tell whether a card is coming before it appears.

In practice

A free kick is given thirty yards out and the referee's arm goes straight up and stays there. Every defender knows the ball has to touch someone else before a goal can count, which is why eleven players line up on the goal line and one attacker taps the ball sideways instead of shooting. Drop the arm instead, and the same free kick produces a shooting gallery and a wall of players ten yards away.

Common misunderstandings

  • The vertical arm is just a delayed free-kick signal. It is specifically the indirect free-kick signal, held up until the ball touches a second player or goes out of play.
  • The assistant's flag direction is arbitrary. The angle encodes the restart — 45 degrees up for an attacking throw, down for a defensive one, level for a goal kick, down at the corner for a corner.
  • Referees invent their own signals. The signals are standardised in the Laws of the Game precisely so a referee and players who share no language can still understand each other.

Quick questions

Why does the referee keep his arm up after an indirect free kick?

To show the kick is still indirect. The arm comes down once the ball has touched a second player or has gone out of play.

What does it mean when the assistant waves the flag in the middle of the pitch?

He is signalling an offence the referee may not have seen — usually a foul or handball away from the referee's position.
