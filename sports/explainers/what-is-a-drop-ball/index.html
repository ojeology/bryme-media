Match mechanics

What is a drop ball?

The short answer

A drop ball is how play restarts after a stoppage with no foul and no clear possession — usually an injury, a referee accidentally interfering, or the ball becoming defective. The referee drops it for one player of the team that last touched it, and everyone else must be four metres away.

The drop ball exists for the awkward moments: play has stopped, nobody has offended, and there is no restart in the law that obviously fits. The referee stops the match, then restarts it by dropping the ball to a player of the team that last touched it before the stoppage. If that contact happened inside the penalty area, the ball is dropped for that team's goalkeeper; anywhere else, it goes to one of that team's outfield players at the spot of the last touch.

The modern version is deliberately simple, and it was not always this way. The old law allowed a contested drop ball between one player from each side — which is exactly why you occasionally saw two players fighting over the ball while everyone else stood around. That also produced the familiar courtesy: the team awarded the drop ball would kick it long towards the opposition goal, and the opposition would let it run out or give it back. IFAB replaced the contest with an uncontested drop to one player, removing both the scuffle and the charade.

Every other player must be at least four metres (about 4.5 yards) away until the ball touches the ground, and the ball is in play the moment it hits the turf — not when it is dropped, and not when someone kicks it. If it goes out of play without anyone touching it, the drop is retaken. If the player who receives it touches it twice before anyone else does, the restart is an indirect free kick to the other team.

Two nuances matter in practice. A goal cannot be scored directly from a drop ball — it must touch a second player first, which is why a player who wins a drop ball and shoots straight at goal will see it disallowed. And a drop ball cannot be used to restart a match after a foul; that is what free kicks are for. Its job is specifically the stoppages that are nobody's fault.

In practice

A player goes down injured with his team in possession near the halfway line. The referee stops play, and on the restart he drops the ball for a player of the team that last touched it; the opposition retreats four metres, the ball bounces once, and play resumes. In the old law the two captains would have contested the drop — and the receiving team would then have hoofed it upfield as a courtesy.

Common misunderstandings

  • A drop ball is contested by two players. Not any more. The law was changed so the referee drops it for a single player of the team that last touched the ball; the contest was removed.
  • You can score directly from a drop ball. No — the ball must touch a second player before a goal can be scored, otherwise it is disallowed.
  • It is used to restart play after a foul. Fouls are restarted with free kicks or penalties. A drop ball is for stoppages that are nobody's fault — injuries, outside interference, a defective ball.

Quick questions

Where is the drop ball taken?

At the place where the ball was last touched before play stopped. If that was inside the penalty area, it is dropped for the goalkeeper of the team that last touched it.

When is the ball actually in play?

The moment it touches the ground after being dropped — not when it leaves the referee's hand, and not when a player kicks it.
