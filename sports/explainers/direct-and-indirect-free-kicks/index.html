Football laws

What is the difference between direct and indirect free kicks?

The short answer

A direct free kick can be scored from directly — it is awarded for fouls like tripping, pushing or handball. An indirect free kick must touch a second player before a goal counts; it is awarded for technical offences like offside, obstruction, dangerous play or a backpass picked up by the keeper.

The distinction is about whether the offence warranted a shot at goal. Fouls that involve contact or a handball are punished with a direct free kick: the offended team can shoot and score straight from it, which is why a foul twenty-five yards out is such a dangerous moment. Referees signal nothing special — the arm goes out horizontally and the kick is live.

Indirect free kicks punish offences about procedure and danger rather than contact: offside, obstruction without contact, playing in a dangerous manner, preventing the goalkeeper from releasing the ball, and the classic backpass offence where a keeper handles a ball deliberately kicked to him by a teammate. The referee signals it by raising one arm straight up and holding it there until the ball touches a second player or goes out of play — that raised arm is the tell every fan should learn.

Scoring rules separate them completely. From an indirect free kick, the ball must be touched by a second player before it enters the goal; if it goes straight in, the restart is a goal kick to the defending team. That is why indirect free kicks near goal produce the famous routine of one player stepping over the ball and a teammate blasting it — the touch is not gamesmanship, it is the law. A direct free kick needs no such touch.

Defenders are bound by the same geometry at both: they must retreat ten yards (9.15 metres) from the ball until it is in play, and the attacking team cannot use the wall's position to manufacture a new offence. Encroachment can be punished with a card, and a quick free kick taken before the wall is set is legal — the attacking team does not have to wait for the referee's whistle unless he has decided to hold the restart for a card or a substitution.

In practice

An indirect free kick eight yards from goal with the entire defensive line standing on the line: one attacker rolls it sideways, a second one thumps it through the gap. Without that sideways touch the goal is disallowed and the defending team restarts with a goal kick. The wall-jumping, the decoy runs and the little square pass are all in service of one sentence in the law — the ball must touch someone else.

Common misunderstandings

  • A goal from an indirect free kick counts if the keeper touches it. It counts if any second player touches it — teammate, opponent, even the keeper. But if it goes in untouched, it is a goal kick, not a goal.
  • The referee's straight-up arm is just a delayed signal. It is the indirect signal, and it stays up until the ball touches a second player or leaves play. It is the clearest way to tell the two kicks apart at a glance.
  • All fouls are direct free kicks. Contact fouls and handball are; technical offences like offside, obstruction and the backpass rule are indirect.

Quick questions

Can you score an own goal directly from an indirect free kick?

If the ball goes into the kicking team's own goal without touching anyone else, the restart is a corner to the opposing team rather than a goal — the same principle applied in reverse.

What is the ten-yard rule?

Opponents must be at least 9.15 metres (10 yards) from the ball at a free kick until it is in play. Referees enforce it, and deliberate encroachment can be cautioned.
