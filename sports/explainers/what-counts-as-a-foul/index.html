Football laws

What counts as a foul in football?

The short answer

A foul is an unfair act by a player against an opponent — kicking, tripping, jumping at, charging, striking, pushing or tackling unfairly — or a handball. The referee judges whether it was careless, reckless or used excessive force, and that judgement decides whether it is no offence, a free kick, a yellow card or a red card.

The law lists the offending actions, but the real decision is the manner: careless, reckless or excessive force. Careless means the player showed a lack of attention — a free kick. Reckless means they acted with complete disregard for the danger to an opponent — a yellow card. Excessive force means they endangered the opponent's safety — a red card. Three identical tackles can produce three different outcomes purely on that scale.

Winning the ball is not a defence by itself. A player can take the ball cleanly and still commit a foul if they went through the opponent to get it, and a player can miss the ball entirely without committing a foul if the challenge was fair. Referees are taught to judge the action, not the outcome, which is why the phrase 'he got the ball' settles so few arguments among fans.

Some offences are technical rather than physical: impeding an opponent without contact, preventing a goalkeeper from releasing the ball, or dissent and delaying the restart. These are punished with indirect free kicks or cautions rather than the direct-kick catalogue. And a foul is only a foul against an opponent — the same list applies to contact with teammates, match officials and substitutes, each with its own restart.

Three sanction levels sit on top of the free kick itself. A yellow card is a caution; two in a match become a red. A straight red removes the player entirely. Some fouls carry a heavier tariff still: a professional foul that denies an obvious goal-scoring opportunity is a red card, but a genuine attempt to play the ball inside the penalty area that results in a penalty is now only a caution — a change introduced specifically to stop a single mistake ending a match.

In practice

A defender slides in from behind and catches an attacker on the ankle, taking the ball a fraction later. Whether that is a yellow, a red or nothing depends entirely on the manner: was the studs-up lunge reckless (yellow), did it endanger the ankle with excessive force (red), or was it a clumsy but controlled attempt (free kick only)? Same contact, three verdicts — the referee is judging the danger, not the bruise.

Common misunderstandings

  • If you win the ball, it cannot be a foul. Winning the ball is not the test. A challenge that goes through an opponent can be a foul even when the ball is taken cleanly.
  • Any foul deserves a card. Most fouls are careless and produce only a free kick. Cards are for recklessness, excessive force, and specific offences like denying a goal-scoring opportunity.
  • Denying a goal-scoring opportunity is always a red card. Inside the penalty area, a genuine attempt to play the ball that results in a penalty is now only a caution. Holding, pulling or pushing without a attempt to play the ball is still a red.

Quick questions

What is the difference between a foul and misconduct?

A foul is an unfair act against an opponent punished with a free kick or penalty; misconduct covers offences like dissent, delaying the restart and unsporting behaviour, punished with cards and an indirect free kick.

Can the referee play on after a foul?

Yes — that is the advantage rule, and the card for the offence is still shown at the next stoppage.
