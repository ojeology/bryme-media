League rules

What happens if teams finish on equal points?

The short answer

When two teams finish level on points, the competition applies a fixed order of tiebreakers — usually goal difference first, then goals scored, with head-to-head records, fair-play points and a play-off further down the list. The order differs between competitions, which is why the same finish can decide different outcomes in different leagues.

Being level on points is not actually a tie in the table — it is a tie in the first column, and every competition writes a fixed queue of tiebreakers for exactly this moment. The teams are never simply declared joint winners: the queue always produces an order, even if it has to reach its very last line.

In most leagues the first tiebreaker is goal difference — goals scored minus goals conceded across the season. England adopted it in 1976-77, replacing goal average (goals scored divided by goals conceded), because the average system punished teams for attacking. The 2011-12 Premier League title is the famous modern case: Manchester City and Manchester United both finished on 89 points, and City took the title on goal difference, +64 to +56.

Some competitions put head-to-head results first instead. Spain's La Liga, Italy's Serie A (for two-team ties) and the group phases of UEFA competitions have all used head-to-head outcomes before goal difference at various points, so a team that took four points off a rival can finish above them even with a worse goal difference. This is the single biggest reason two leagues can order identical records differently.

If the first tiebreakers are still identical, the queue goes deeper: goals scored is the common second test, then away goals in some historical formats, then fair-play points (based on cards received), and finally — if the position decides the title, relegation or qualification — a play-off at a neutral venue. If nothing important hangs on the position, some competitions leave it decided by the deeper administrative criteria and the table simply records an order.

In practice

English football has produced the perfect pair of examples. In 1988-89 Arsenal and Liverpool finished level on points AND level on goal difference — Arsenal won the title on goals scored, 73 to 65, with the decisive goal coming in the last minute of the season at Anfield. Twenty-three years later City and United finished level on points with different goal differences, and City won on that margin. Same league, same tie in the first column, two completely different tiebreakers deciding the title.

Common misunderstandings

  • Teams that finish level on points share the title or the position. No — the competition's tiebreaker queue always produces a single order, and a play-off is used as the last resort when something is at stake.
  • Goal difference is always the first tiebreaker. It is the most common, but several major leagues and every UEFA group phase have used head-to-head results first.
  • The tiebreakers only matter on the final day. They are live all season — the table is ordered by them after every match, and they shape late-season incentives, like chasing a fourth goal instead of settling for 2-0.

Quick questions

What happens if teams are level on points AND goal difference?

The queue continues: usually goals scored next, then head-to-head, then fair-play points, and finally a play-off at a neutral ground if the position decides something important.

Can two teams be declared joint champions?

In practice no. Even competitions with the deepest queues finish with a defined order; a play-off is the catch-all when every listed criterion is equal.
