Knockout football

How do penalty shootouts work?

The short answer

Five kickers from each team take penalties alternately, and the team that scores more from its five wins. If they are still level it goes to sudden death — one pair at a time until one team scores and the other misses — and only players eligible at the final whistle can take part.

A shootout is its own mini-competition with its own rules. The referee tosses a coin and the winner chooses whether to kick first; each manager submits an ordered list of takers; and the kicks alternate — first team, second team, five each. If one side is already uncatchable before all five are taken, the shootout ends there. Order is strategic, not fixed: your best taker often goes first or last, and coaches study who handles pressure best.

The goalkeeper's obligations are strict. He must stay on his line until the ball is kicked — at least part of one foot on, in line with, or behind the goal line at the moment of contact — and he cannot touch the kicker or the ball beyond the permitted dive. The kicker cannot touch the ball twice, and the ball must be kicked forward. Encroachment by either side leads to a retake, and a keeper who moves early and saves will have the save wiped out by a retake.

The biggest misconception about shootouts is the rebound. In open play a saved penalty stays live and a striker can follow up — in a shootout, nobody else is allowed near the box and the kicker cannot play the ball twice, so a follow-up tap-in is impossible. The only way a rebound counts is when the ball goes in off the goalkeeper or the woodwork without anyone else touching it. Rebounds, deflections and keepers wandering up for a header are open-play luxuries: the shootout is stripped to its purest form.

There is one fairness rule people rarely notice: if the teams have unequal numbers of eligible players — for instance after a red card — both teams reduce to equal numbers before the shootout begins. The shootout is officially recorded as a draw in the match record, with the tie decided by the shootout result — which is why the history books say a final finished 1-1 and was won 'on penalties'.

In practice

A shootout is level at 3-3 after five kicks each. Sudden death begins with round six: the first taker scores, the second must score to survive. He strikes it low to the keeper's right, the keeper gets a fingertip to it — save. The shootout ends there, but the match record shows a 1-1 draw with a shootout win, and the keeper, who never touched the ball in normal time, is the story of the night.

Common misunderstandings

  • You can score from the rebound of a saved shootout penalty. No — in a shootout nobody else may touch the ball, and the kicker cannot touch it twice. The kick ends once it is saved or stops; only a deflection in off the keeper or the woodwork counts as a goal.
  • The best five takers must take the first five penalties in order. Any eligible player can be listed in any order. Managers often split the best takers across the five rounds rather than fielding them consecutively.
  • The shootout result counts in the match score. The match is officially recorded as a draw; the shootout decides who advances and is recorded separately.

Quick questions

Can a goalkeeper take a penalty in a shootout?

Yes — any eligible player can be listed, including the goalkeeper, though managers rarely do it because the list must include enough takers to reach sudden death.

Who picks the order of takers?

The manager, usually with the captain, submits a numbered list before the shootout begins. The list can be reordered only before the shootout starts, not mid-shootout.
