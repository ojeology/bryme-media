Football laws

What is an own goal?

The short answer

An own goal is scored when a player puts the ball into his own team's net. It counts for the opposition — but the credit and the statistics depend on whether the ball was already heading towards the goal: a deflection is usually credited to the attacker's shot, while a genuinely misplaced clearance becomes an own goal.

The basic case is simple: the ball crosses the line, it went in off a defending player, and the goal is awarded to the attacking team. The law does not care whose foot it came off or whether the defender meant it — what matters is that the whole ball crossed the whole line without any attacking offence in the build-up.

The interesting part is attribution, because it decides statistics and occasionally arguments about a player's record. If a shot was already on target and a defender's touch merely deflects it past his own keeper, most competitions credit the goal to the attacker — the shot was going in anyway. If a defender miscontrols a pass, slices a clearance or heads a cross into his own net from an angle where the ball was never going in, it is an own goal. The distinction is judgement, and the two official bodies that track such things sometimes disagree on the same incident.

Own goals have their own restart: the game resumes with a kick-off to the team that conceded, exactly as it would after any other goal. There are also two edge cases worth knowing. A goal cannot be scored directly into a team's own net from a throw-in, a goal kick or a corner — those situations produce a corner to the opposition instead. And an own goal can be disallowed for the same reasons any goal can: an offside or a foul in the build-up wipes it out just as it would at the other end.

Video review has made one aspect cleaner and another murkier. It is now easier to see whether a touch came off the attacker or the defender, and whether the shot was on target; but the judgement about whether the ball would have gone in regardless remains a matter of interpretation. Either way, the match is decided the same — the goal stands, and it counts against the team that put it there.

In practice

A low cross flashes across the six-yard box. A defender slides in and turns it past his own keeper from two yards: own goal, no debate — the ball was never going in on its own. Now take the same cross deflected by the defender off a striker's shot that was already beating the keeper: most competitions give the goal to the striker, because the shot was on target before the touch.

Common misunderstandings

  • Any goal that goes in off a defender is an own goal. A touch that merely deflects an already on-target shot is usually credited to the attacker. Own goals are for genuinely misplaced clearances and touches where the ball was not going in.
  • The defender is punished or booked. There is no sanction. An own goal is simply a goal for the opposition, and the game restarts with a kick-off.
  • You can score an own goal directly from a corner or throw-in. No — from those restarts the result is a corner to the opposition rather than a goal.

Quick questions

Can an own goal be disallowed?

Yes — for exactly the same reasons as any other goal: offside, a foul or a handball by an attacking player in the build-up.

Does an own goal affect a player's goal statistics?

It is recorded separately and does not count as a goal for the player who scored it, which is why goal tallies sometimes differ between sources after a deflected finish.
