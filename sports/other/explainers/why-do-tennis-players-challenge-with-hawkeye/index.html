Why do tennis players challenge with Hawk-Eye?

Evergreen • Updated 02 Oct 2026 • 3 min read
Answer: Hawk-Eye in tennis is 10 cameras tracking the ball at 340 fps to the millimetre, and the challenge lets a player ask the system to overrule the line judge — 3 challenges per set, 1 extra at tiebreak, correct keeps the challenge, wrong loses it — with an average 25% overturn rate because the human eye misses by 3-5mm at 130 mph.

Explained properly

Hawk-Eye is 10 high-speed cameras around the court triangulating the ball's centre at 340 frames per second to about 2.6mm accuracy, then rendering the bounce as a graphic in 10 seconds — the line judge calls in 0.3 seconds at 130 mph, Hawk-Eye confirms in 10 seconds at 2.6mm, which is why the challenge exists — the eye cannot see 3mm at 130 mph, the system can to 2.6mm, and the biggest error is on the line where the eye's parallax is worst.

The challenge explains the number. Three per set, plus one at tiebreak, with correct challenges retained — so a player who challenges three times and is right three times still has three, while three wrongs leave zero and a wrong at 30-40 can cost the game — the 3 is the bottleneck that stops every point being challenged, which would add 10 seconds per point and make a 3-hour match 4 hours.

On-court vs live explains the show. The stadium screen shows the bounce in slow motion with the mark — in for a 1mm, out for a 2mm — and the crowd roars at the graphic, which is why the challenge is the most televised 10 seconds in tennis — the player's challenge is the fan's replay, and the umpire's call stands if Hawk-Eye is unavailable, which is why some Challengers with no Hawk-Eye still use the human call only.

The other Hawk-Eye is offside. The same Hawk-Eye company does football's goal-line 500Hz watch, but tennis's 340 fps is ball vs line, not ball vs defender — the tennis bounce is the decision, the football goal-line is the sensor, and the tennis challenge is player-initiated while football's GLT is automatic, which is why tennis players control the challenge and football players do not.

The evergreen myth is the mark. On clay the ball leaves a mark, so Roland Garros did not use Hawk-Eye until 2024 on small courts because the mark was the judge and the umpire would walk to check it — the 3mm mark on clay is visible, on hard it is not, so hard and grass use Hawk-Eye and the player challenges, clay used the mark and the umpire, which is why the 2024 Roland Garros Hawk-Eye on hard courts was the last clay holdout.

Picture this: US Open: Swiatek serves at 120 mph on break point, the line judge calls out, Swiatek challenges with 2 left, Hawk-Eye shows the ball's centre 2mm inside the line at 340 fps — in, correct, challenge stays at 2, point replayed as let if the call was out before the rally, or awarded if the out call was late — the 10-second 2.6mm graphic that overturns the 0.3-second human at 130 mph, 3 per set, 25% overturn.

Myths vs reality

“Hawk-Eye is always right.”
Reality: About 2.6mm accuracy at 340 fps — the 3mm mark on clay is the human vs system margin, which is why the overturn is 25% not 50%.
“Players have unlimited challenges.”
Reality: 3 per set plus 1 at tiebreak, correct keeps, wrong loses — three wrongs and no challenges left for the tiebreak, so the 3 is the bottleneck.
“Hawk-Eye is just for TV.”
Reality: The player's challenge triggers the 10-second graphic that overrules the line judge — the player, not TV, initiates the overrule.

FAQ

How many challenges in tennis?

3 per set plus 1 at tiebreak — correct keeps, wrong loses, so three wrongs leave zero for the rest of the set.

How accurate is Hawk-Eye?

About 2.6mm at 340 frames per second — the human eye misses by 3-5mm at 130 mph, so 25% are overturned.

Does clay use Hawk-Eye?

Hard and grass yes, clay used the mark until 2024 — the ball's mark on clay is visible, so the umpire walked to the mark, now Hawk-Eye is on all courts.
