What are the four Grand Slams in tennis?

Evergreen • Updated 02 Oct 2026 • 3 min read
Answer: The four Grand Slams are the four majors — Australian Open (hard, Jan), Roland Garros (clay, May), Wimbledon (grass, July), US Open (hard, Aug) — each 128 players, best of 5 men, best of 3 women, 2000 ranking points to the winner, and the calendar Slam is winning all four in one year, last done by Rod Laver 1969 men and Steffi Graf 1988 women.

Explained properly

The Slams are the majors because they are ITF not ATP — the ITF owns them, the ATP owns the Masters 1000, so the Slams have 128 players, 32 seeds, 64 first-round losers who still get $80k, and 2000 points vs 1000 for a Masters. The surfaces make the cycle: Melbourne hard is medium-fast, Paris clay is slow and high-bouncing, Wimbledon grass is fast and low, New York hard is fast with humidity — the same player must win on three surfaces in seven months to hold all four.

The season explains the order. Australian summer hard in January tests heat, Roland Garros clay in late May tests stamina with 5-hour rallies, Wimbledon grass two weeks later tests serve-and-volley transition on a surface that is green on Monday and brown by the semi, US hard in late August tests humidity and night matches under lights — the surface change is the evergreen reason a calendar Slam is rare, not just talent.

Best of 5 vs 3 decides the men's vs women's narrative. Men need 3 sets at Slams, women 2, so a men's 5-set can be 5 hours and a women's 2-0 can be 58 minutes — the Slams' best-of-5 for men is the only best-of-5 left after the Tour finals went best-of-3, which is why men's Slams produce comebacks from two sets down that women's cannot, and why the men's final is Sunday and the women's Saturday.

The calendar Slam vs career Slam myth matters. A calendar Slam is all four in one year — Laver 1969, Graf 1988 — a career Slam is one of each over a career — Djokovic, Nadal, Federer and Serena all have the career, not the calendar — and the Golden Slam is all four plus Olympic gold in one year, only Graf 1988, which is why the calendar remains the hardest evergreen, not the career.

The Australian heat rule shows the operation. Melbourne can suspend at 40°C heat stress, Roland Garros has a retractable roof on Philippe Chatrier since 2020, Wimbledon has a roof on Centre and No.1, US Open has two roofs — the Slams' roofs decide if the final finishes under lights at midnight, which happened at the US Open 2021 when rain pushed the women's semi to Arthur Ashe roof and the final to Sunday.

Picture this: 2023: Djokovic won Australian hard, lost Roland Garros semi, won Wimbledon grass, lost US final — 3 of 4, not the calendar, while the women's 2023 was Sabalenka Australian, Swiatek Roland Garros, Vondrousova Wimbledon, Gauff US — four different winners, no calendar, which is why the Slams' 128-draw 2000-point four-surface cycle is the evergreen chase that 3 of 4 still feels like failure.

Myths vs reality

“The Grand Slams are ATP events.”
Reality: ITF majors with 128 players and 2000 points — ATP Masters are 56-96 players and 1000 points, so the Slams are the ITF's four, not the Tour's.
“All Slams are on hard courts.”
Reality: Australian and US are hard, Roland Garros clay, Wimbledon grass — three surfaces in seven months, which is why the calendar Slam is rare.
“A career Slam is the same as a calendar.”
Reality: Career is one of each over a career, calendar is all four in one year — only Laver men and Graf women have the calendar in the Open Era.

FAQ

Which are the four Grand Slams?

Australian Open hard Jan, Roland Garros clay May, Wimbledon grass July, US Open hard Aug — 128 players, best of 5 men, best of 3 women, 2000 points.

Who won the calendar Slam?

Rod Laver 1969 men (all four plus the calendar), Steffi Graf 1988 women plus Olympic gold for the Golden Slam — no man since Laver, no woman since Graf.

Do all Slams have roofs?

Centre courts do now — Rod Laver, Chatrier, Centre and Ashe — so finals finish under lights even in rain, unlike the 2010 Monday final.
