How does overtime work in basketball?

Evergreen • Updated 02 Oct 2026 • 3 min read
Answer: If the scores are level at the end of regulation, the game goes into overtime — an extra period of five minutes, repeated as many times as needed until someone leads. Fouls carry over, timeouts are limited, and the pace becomes slower and more deliberate as fatigue sets in.

Explained properly

Basketball cannot end level, so a tied game simply continues. Regulation is four quarters of ten minutes under FIBA rules and four of twelve in the NBA; if it is level when the horn sounds, both teams play an additional five-minute period. If that period also ends level, another is played — there is no limit, and the longest professional games have run to six overtimes.

What carries over matters as much as the extra clock. Personal fouls accumulated during regulation stay on the players, so anyone in foul trouble remains in trouble, and in the NBA the team foul count resets for the overtime period rather than continuing. Timeouts are reduced: teams get a small allocation for the extra period plus any they have left unused, which is why a team that hoarded timeouts late in regulation has a real advantage.

The character of the game changes. Five minutes is long enough to be deliberate but short enough that every possession is magnified — teams run fewer transition plays, use more of the clock, and lean on their best player. Coaches who managed their stars' minutes well in regulation are rewarded, because fatigue makes the difference between a made and a missed shot more than any tactical adjustment at that stage.

Different competitions handle the details differently. Playoff and knockout games use the same five-minute periods until someone wins. Some summer leagues and tournaments have used a target-score ending — first team to a set number of points above the opponent wins — as an alternative to a fixed-length overtime, because it produces a decisive finish rather than another period of basketball. That variation has not been adopted for the standard professional game.

Picture this: A game is tied at 104 with four seconds left; the shot rims out and the horn sounds. Five more minutes follow, and a player who picked up his fifth foul in the third quarter is still one foul from disqualification — he has to defend without fouling against a team that has all its timeouts. The team that won the game is usually the one that managed the clock and its fouls best in the twenty minutes before overtime began.

Myths vs reality

“Overtime is sudden death.”
Reality: It is a full five-minute period. The game is only decided when a period ends with one team ahead, so multiple overtimes are possible.
“Fouls reset at the start of overtime.”
Reality: Personal fouls carry over, so players stay in foul trouble. Only the team foul count resets in the NBA.
“Teams get a full new set of timeouts.”
Reality: Overtime brings a reduced allocation, plus whatever a team has left unused — which is why saving timeouts is a real strategy.

FAQ

How long is an overtime period?

Five minutes in the NBA, under FIBA rules and in college basketball. Additional periods are played until one team leads at the end of one.

Has a game ever needed more than one overtime?

Yes — many. The longest NBA game ran to six overtimes, and triple-overtime games are a recurring feature of both regular seasons and playoffs.
