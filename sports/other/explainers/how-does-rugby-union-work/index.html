How does rugby union actually work?

Evergreen • Updated 02 Oct 2026 • 3 min read
Answer: Rugby union is 15-a-side, 80 minutes (2x40), where you carry not dribble, must pass backwards, tackle to the ground, and score 5 points for a try in the in-goal, 2 for the conversion kick, 3 for a penalty kick and 3 for a drop goal — the scrum and lineout are the set pieces that have no football equivalent.

Explained properly

The backward pass explains the shape. Rugby is carried, not dribbled, and the ball must be passed backwards or flat, never forward — a forward pass is a scrum to the other team — so the team moves forward by running through the gain line, not by passing forward, which is why the 10-12-13 centres run the crash and the 9-10 pivot pass back to the wing.

The tackle explains the contest. A tackle brings the ball carrier to ground, the carrier must release, and both sides compete for the ball over it — the ruck — where the tackler must roll away and the arriving players must stay on their feet and enter through the gate behind the hindmost foot, so the breakdown is a second contest after the tackle, not a foul, and clean ball from the ruck lets the scrum-half pass.

The scrum is the restart for minor infringements. Eight forwards per side bind — 3 front row of loosehead, hooker, tighthead, 2 locks, 2 flankers, 1 number 8 — and the scrum-half feeds the ball mid-tunnel, the hooker hooks, and the pack pushes on the referee's crouch-bind-set, with penalties for early push or wheeling — the scrum is a shoving contest that can win a penalty 30 metres out, which is why South Africa pick 6 locks on the bench as the Bomb Squad.

The lineout is the throw. When the ball goes out, the hooker throws 5-15m between two lines of jumpers lifted by teammates, with the jumper who catches forming a maul — a driving bind — that can march 20m, so the lineout is the platform for the rolling maul try that looks illegal but is legal if the ball stays at the back and the maul does not collapse.

Scoring is 5+2+3+3. A try is 5 for grounding the ball in the 10-22m in-goal, conversion is 2 from the spot in line with the try 20m out, penalty 3 through the H posts, drop goal 3 on the run — a 5-point try plus 2 makes 7, so a 3-point kick is less than half a try, which is why teams chase tries at 14-12 down late, not kicks, and why the Six Nations table has 4 points for a win plus bonus for 4 tries or losing by 7.

Discipline is cards with teeth. A yellow is 10 minutes in the sin bin with 14 men, a red is 14 men for the rest after the 2023 law — and since 2023 a 20-minute red lets the player be replaced after 20, so the game is 15 vs 15 again but the sent-off player is gone — the 20-minute red is the bottleneck that changed 6 Nations discipline more than any law since.

Picture this: Six Nations at Twickenham: England vs France, lineout 10m from the line, England jumper catches, 8-man maul forms, drives 12m, scrum-half passes to the 9 who dives for the try — 5 points, conversion from the touchline for 2 makes 7, France reply with a drop goal on 78 for 3 to make 7-3, England win with 7 vs 3 — the 5+2 vs 3 arithmetic that decides every rugby game.

Myths vs reality

“Rugby is just tackling football.”
Reality: Tackle to ground plus ruck plus backward pass — the ball must be released after the tackle and contested on feet through the gate, not dribbled or passed forward.
“A try is 6 points.”
Reality: 5, plus 2 for the conversion kick — 7 total, less than half for a penalty's 3, so tries are chased over kicks.
“Scrums are just restarts.”
Reality: A scrum is a pushing contest that wins penalties 30m out — South Africa's Bomb Squad of 6 locks wins games at the scrum alone.

FAQ

How many players in rugby union?

15 per side, 80 minutes (2x40), 8 subs, 5-point tries, 2 for conversions, 3 for penalties and drop goals.

What is a ruck?

The contest after the tackle where the ball carrier must release and both sides compete on their feet through the gate — clean ball lets the scrum-half pass.

What is the Bomb Squad?

South Africa's 6-2 bench split with 6 forwards who win scrums and mauls in the second half — a World Cup-winning tactic.
