How does LBW work in cricket?

Evergreen • Updated 02 Oct 2026 • 3 min read
Answer: LBW is leg before wicket — the batter is out if the ball would have hit the stumps but the pad blocked it — with three checks: pitched in line or outside off, impact in line, and hitting the stumps, plus the DRS ball-tracking that overturns the on-field call on umpire's call margins.

Explained properly

LBW is the 10% that confuses. Bowled is hit stumps, caught is hit then caught, lbw is would have hit stumps but pad intervened — the bowler appeals, the umpire judges pitched, impact and hitting, all in 2 seconds, with 0.01 on the edge deciding, which is why lbw is the only dismissal that needs prediction, not just observation, and why the umpire's finger stays down more often than up — 30% of appeals are given lbw, 70% not out.

The three checks decide. Pitched: the ball must have pitched in line between wicket and wicket or outside off — pitched outside leg is never lbw, even if it would have hit, which is why a leg-spinner pitching outside leg and hitting the pad in front of middle is not out. Impact: the pad's first contact must be in line between wicket and wicket, or outside off where the batter was not playing a shot — outside off with a shot offered is not lbw, without a shot it can be, which is why the umpire checks the shot.

Hitting is the third and the myth. The ball must be hitting the stumps — middle and leg, or leg and middle — by more than half the ball, otherwise the tracking shows umpire's call — the on-field call stands if it is nail-biting. That is why the DRS graphic shows red for out, green for not, and yellow for umpire's call — yellow keeps the on-field, red or green overturns, and the width of the yellow is the 50% that creates the controversy.

The DRS review explains the overturn. The batter reviews with the team losing one review per innings if failed, the ball-tracking from 6 cameras predicts the path after the pad, and the third umpire checks pitched, impact and hitting with the ball-tracking — the on-field umpire's soft signal is the base, and the tracking's 2.5mm accuracy decides — the 2023 change to umpire's call on height kept more on-fields, which is why height is now the most common umpire's call, not line.

Why lbw took 100 years is the pad. Before 1935 lbw was rare because batters could kick the ball away outside off with the pad and be not out — Don Bradman averaged 99 that way — then law 36 was changed to allow lbw even with impact outside off if no shot was offered, so the pad could no longer be a second bat — the evergreen law that a batter who pads outside off without offering a shot can be lbw is the 1935 fix, not the original.

Evergreen, the 10% explains the game. LBW is the only dismissal where the batter can be out without the bat or the ball hitting the stumps — the prediction of hitting — which is why the DRS, the umpire's call, and the pitched-outside-leg exception are the cricket equivalent of football's offside frame and line — the 10% that decides 30% of debates.

Picture this: India vs Australia: Lyon bowls off-spin that pitches outside off, turns, hits the pad in line middle-and-leg with no shot offered, the on-field gives not out, Australia reviews, tracking shows pitched outside off yes, impact in line yes, hitting leg and middle red for out — overturned to out on umpire's call's 50% — the 3-check plus ball-tracking that football's VAR offside line also needs, but with pitched outside leg as the exception that football has no parallel for.

Myths vs reality

“Any pad that blocks the stumps is lbw.”
Reality: Three checks — pitched in line or outside off, impact in line (or outside off with no shot), and hitting — pitched outside leg is never lbw even if it would have hit.
“DRS overrules clearly.”
Reality: Yellow is umpire's call — if hitting is 50% on the stumps, the on-field stands — red or green overturns, yellow keeps the finger as it was.
“The batter must be playing a shot.”
Reality: If impact is outside off and no shot is offered, lbw can still be given — the 1935 law to stop pad as a second bat.

FAQ

What does LBW stand for?

Leg before wicket — the pad blocked a ball that would have hit the stumps, judged on pitched, impact and hitting.

What is umpire's call?

The yellow zone where hitting is 50% on the stumps — the on-field call stands, not overturned, to keep the umpire's decision central.

Can LBW be pitched outside leg?

No — pitched outside leg is never lbw, even if it would have hit — the only pitched exception in the law.
