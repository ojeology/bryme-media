Sam Mendes' 1917 follows two young British soldiers sent across no man's land to deliver orders that will stop 1,600 men from walking into a trap. The film was staged and edited to look like one continuous take, so the audience stays with them — through trenches, farmhouse ruins and a night-time river — without ever cutting away.

Roger Deakins' cinematography won the film its reputation: the illusion is never broken, and the camera's refusal to blink turns a familiar war-story errand into something closer to a survival game at ground level.

Why it is worth your evening

The technique is the point, and it serves emotion rather than showing off. Because there are no cuts, small moments — a soldier checking a watch, a field of cherry blossoms — land with a weight that cutting would dissolve. It is the best recent argument that form itself can be the protagonist.

How to watch it

Rentable on major digital stores and streaming on rotating services. Rated for intense war violence and some distressing scenes; the single-take style makes it unusually immersive, which is worth knowing before a late-night watch.
